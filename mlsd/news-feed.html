Chapter 10

Personalized News Feed

A social feed shows each user the posts from friends, pages, and groups they are most likely to value. A user who follows a few hundred sources has far more unseen posts than time, so order matters more than membership. The core idea is a multi-stage funnel (retrieve, rank, re-rank) whose ranker predicts several kinds of reaction per post and blends those predictions into one score using weights that encode product values.

Clarifying Requirements

The feed looks like a simple sorted list, but almost every requirement changes the design: what counts as a candidate, which reactions we can learn from, and how much of the page we may compute per request. Spend the first few minutes pinning these down.

Build a ranked, personalized feed of unseen posts and posts with unseen comments for about 500 million daily users, to maximize engagement. Posts contain text and media; users react in several ways, and many react rarely. Harmful content must be demoted, new users and authors must work, and the first page must load in a few hundred milliseconds.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

"Engagement" is a business word, not a training label. The first design decision is which observable behavior the model should predict. A candidate may weigh three families of options.

Candidate objectives for the feed ranker
ObjectiveProsCons
Maximize implicit signals (clicks, dwell time)Plentiful, including for passive users; fast feedbackRewards clickbait, sensational posts, and long videos regardless of satisfaction
Maximize explicit signals (likes, comments, shares)Clear intent; closer to "this was worth my time"Sparse (well under a few percent of impressions); passive users give almost none; easy to game with engagement bait
Predict each reaction, then blend into one value scoreUses dense and sparse signals together; weights express product values and can change without retraining; negative signals (hide) join the same formulaNeeds calibrated probabilities and a process to choose weights
Predict a single learned "engagement" score end to endOne model, one labelHides what is being optimized; cannot retune the mix; label must still be hand-built from the same reactions

Predict several reaction probabilities and combine them linearly: value = ∑ wk · P(reaction k), with a negative weight for hides. The model answers "what will this user do?"; the weights answer "what do we value?". Keeping those two questions separate is the central design idea.

Why blend instead of training one engagement score? There are four reasons worth saying aloud. First, reactions mean different things: a comment usually reflects more investment than a click. Second, they have very different base rates, so a single pooled label would be dominated by the most frequent reaction. Third, product teams change priorities (more conversation, fewer videos) and should not need to retrain. Fourth, negative feedback such as hide has to enter the objective with a minus sign; a pooled "engaged or not" label cannot express that. The linear form also has a convenient property: a reaction a user almost never performs gets a probability near zero, so it automatically has little influence on that user's ranking.

Weights come from two sources, used together. Surveys and holdout studies tell the team which interactions people call meaningful, which gives a starting ordering of weights. Online experiments then test several weight vectors as parallel arms and choose the one that improves long-term metrics (retention, survey quality) while keeping guardrails (hides, reports) flat. Mature systems treat the weights as a small search problem, sometimes with constrained or Bayesian optimization, and may personalize them for users whose behavior differs, but a fixed global vector is the right starting answer.

Post A: 0.30·1 + 0.35·1 + 0.20·2 + 0.06·5 + 0.02·6 − 0.005·20 = 0.30 + 0.35 + 0.40 + 0.30 + 0.12 − 0.10 = 1.37. Post B: 0.45 + 0.40 + 0.20 + 0.05 + 0.18 − 0.60 = 0.68. Post C: 0.20 + 0.25 + 0.16 + 0.60 + 0.06 − 0.08 = 1.19. Final order: A, C, B. Sorting by P(click) alone would have put B first.

The blend only works if each probability is calibrated (a predicted 0.20 really means 20% of such impressions get that reaction). A head that over-predicts shares by 3x silently triples the share weight. Calibration is a requirement, not polish; see the Evaluation section.

Input and output

The model's input is a viewer, a candidate post, and the context of the request. Its output is a vector of reaction probabilities for that pair. The ranker applies the blend to every candidate and sorts.

ML category: learning to rank

Ranking can be learned in three ways. They differ in what one training example is and what the loss compares.

Learning-to-rank families
FamilyTraining exampleProsCons
PointwiseOne (viewer, post) pair with a label per reactionSimple; scales to billions of examples; probabilities plug straight into the blend; easy to calibrateIgnores the other posts shown; does not optimize list order directly
PairwiseTwo posts, label "which was preferred"Directly trains order; robust to score offsetsNeeds trustworthy preference pairs; scores are not probabilities, so the weighted blend loses meaning
ListwiseA whole list with a list-level lossOptimizes metrics such as nDCGExpensive, hard to train, and sensitive to position bias in logged lists

Decision. Use a pointwise, multi-task model. The weighted blend needs calibrated probabilities, and only the pointwise formulation produces them. Order-aware effects (diversity, spacing) are handled afterward by a re-ranking pass that sees the whole list.

Three logging details matter. First, log an impression only when the post was actually on screen (for example, at least 500 ms in the viewport); posts that were ranked but never scrolled into view are not negatives. Second, log position and the full feature vector used at serving time, so training sees what the ranker saw, not a later recomputation. Third, log reactions with the same request_id as the impression so labels can be joined within a window.

Feature engineering

Features are organized by the entity they describe. The ranker scores (viewer, post) pairs, so it needs the viewer, the post, and, most importantly, the relationship between the two.

Affinity features are usually the strongest signal in a social feed: people react most to people they know well. They are computed as smoothed rates (reactions divided by impressions of that author, with a prior so one lucky like does not look like a 100% rate) over several windows. Because the table is viewer x author, it is sparse: most pairs have no history, which is where content features and the viewer's overall behavior must carry the prediction.

Post age needs care. Fresh posts get more reactions because they are at the top of friends' feeds and because stale news matters less, so age is a strong feature, but its effect differs by relationship: a close friend's engagement-announcement is still welcome a day later, while a news page's headline is not. Feeding age as a feature (rather than hard-coding a decay) lets the model learn these curves. A hand-written decay is still useful as a prior in the pass-0 retrieval scorer.

In an interview, state that every feature must be available at serving time with the same definition used in training. For fast-changing features such as post counters, log the value the ranker saw rather than recomputing it later; otherwise the model trains on information from the future and looks great offline but disappoints online.

Labels

Each reaction head needs its own label. Three sources are plausible.

Label sources
SourceDefinitionProsCons
Explicit reactionsPositive if the user liked, commented, or shared within a window (say 24 hours) of the impressionClean intent; direct input to the blendSparse; passive users contribute almost no positives
Implicit behaviorPositive if dwell exceeds a threshold, the user expanded the text, or watched at least half a videoDense for every user, including passive onesThreshold choices are arbitrary; long or attention-grabbing posts look good
Negative feedbackPositive for the "hide" head if the user hid the post, unfollowed the author, or reported itCaptures harm and annoyance that engagement hidesVery rare; reporting is not uniform across user groups

Decision. Build all three: explicit heads for like, comment, and share; implicit heads for click and dwell; and a negative head for hide. A shown post is positive for a head if that reaction occurred within the window and negative otherwise. Dwell is binarized with a threshold (for example 10 seconds, chosen so that it separates real reads from quick scrolls, and ideally normalized by content type because a text post and a video have different natural dwell times).

Class imbalance is severe and differs by head. Commenting and sharing happen on a fraction of a percent of impressions, while dwell is common. The standard remedies are to down-sample negatives (and recalibrate afterward, see below), to weight rare positives, and to share representations across heads so a rare head borrows strength from a common one.

Several biases contaminate these labels, and a good answer names them.

  • Selection bias. We only observe reactions to posts we chose to show. A post the old ranker never surfaced has no label, so the new model can never learn it is good. Reserve a small exploration slice (randomized or boosted exposure) and use it for unbiased training and evaluation.
  • Position bias. Slot 1 gets more reactions than slot 8 for the same post. Training on raw labels teaches the model that "posts that were ranked high are good". Add position as a training-only input (or a separate shallow tower, as in the multitask ranker of Zhao et al.) and set it to a constant at serving, or use inverse-propensity weights.
  • Delayed feedback. Comments and shares can arrive hours after the impression. Training on yesterday's log undercounts them. Wait for the label window to close, or use a model that corrects for incomplete labels.
  • Noisy implicit labels. A long dwell can mean fascination or distraction. Pair dwell with explicit and survey signals when tuning thresholds.
  • Time-based splits. Train on days D-30 through D-2, validate on D-1, test on D. A random split leaks future counters and trending posts into training.

Model Development

Model options

Model families for the ranker
OptionStrengthsWeaknesses
Logistic regression with crosses (one per head)Fast, interpretable, easy to calibrate; strong baselineManual feature crosses; cannot use raw embeddings well; one model per reaction to maintain
Gradient-boosted trees (per head)Strong on tabular features; little feature scalingPoor with high-dimensional embeddings; hard to share across tasks; slow retraining on huge logs
Deep network with shared bottom and per-reaction headsLearns feature interactions; ingests embeddings; one model serves all heads; rare heads borrow strengthHeads can interfere when tasks conflict; more serving cost
Multi-gate mixture-of-experts (MMoE) networkExperts specialize; per-task gates reduce interference between conflicting tasksMore parameters and tuning; gates can collapse onto one expert
Sequential transformer over the user's interaction historyCaptures taste trajectory; helps sparse usersCostly to train and serve; needs GPU serving and shared-context batching

Decision. Start with a shared-bottom multi-task deep network, move to MMoE if offline results show task conflict, and mention a sequential model as the next step. Meta describes multitask neural networks as the core of News Feed ranking, and LinkedIn describes MMoE heads (with DCNv2 experts) on top of a sequential encoder in its recent feed system.

Sharing the trunk is both a cost and a modeling choice. It is cheaper to serve than six separate models, and it lets sparse heads (share, hide) use representations learned mostly from click and dwell. The risk is negative transfer: a hide head and a click head may pull the trunk in opposite directions for sensational posts. MMoE addresses this by replacing the single trunk with several experts and giving each task its own gate, a softmax over experts that decides how much each expert contributes to that task's tower (Ma et al. 2018). Related tasks can share experts; conflicting tasks can route to different ones.

Passive users

A passive user may scroll for twenty minutes and never tap like. Explicit heads see almost no positives for that user, so predictions are uninformative and the blend collapses to ordering by popularity. Three techniques address this, and a candidate should offer them together.

  • Implicit-signal heads. Dwell, expand-text, video watch fraction, and profile visits fire for everyone. Include them in the blend so a passive user's ordering is driven by what they pause on.
  • Shared representations. The trunk learns patterns from active users (for example, close-friend photos are engaging) and applies them to passive users with similar features.
  • Viewer-conditioned weights. For users with little explicit history, shift blend weight toward implicit heads; for active users, explicit heads carry more of the weight. This can be a rule or a small learned function of the viewer's activity level.

Training

Loss. The total loss is the sum of binary cross-entropy over heads, optionally weighted per task:

L = ∑k λk · BCE(yk, pk), where BCE = −[ y log p + (1−y) log(1−p) ] averaged over examples.

In plain words: each head is graded on its own binary question and the grades are added, so the shared layers receive gradients from every task. The weights λk control how loudly each task speaks to the trunk; they are not the blend weights. Rare heads often get larger λ so they are not drowned out.

One example, three heads, all λ = 1. Labels (click, like, hide) = (1, 0, 0) with predictions (0.8, 0.1, 0.02). Click: −ln 0.8 = 0.223. Like: −ln(1−0.1) = 0.105. Hide: −ln(1−0.02) = 0.020. Total loss = 0.223 + 0.105 + 0.020 = 0.348.

Calibration after down-sampling. Keeping only a fraction w of negatives speeds up training but inflates predicted probabilities. If the model outputs p under negative keep-rate w, the corrected probability is q = p / (p + (1 − p) / w).

Suppose a head has a true rate of 4%: of 100 impressions, 4 are positive and 96 negative. Keeping 10% of negatives leaves 4 positives and 9.6 negatives, so the trained model learns an average output of 4 / 13.6 = 0.294. Correcting with w = 0.1 gives q = 0.294 / (0.294 + 0.706 / 0.1) = 0.294 / 7.353 = 0.040, which restores the true 4%.

Even after this correction, networks tend to be miscalibrated, particularly in the tails, so a per-head calibrator (isotonic regression or Platt scaling fit on a recent held-out day) is applied on top. Guo et al. showed that modern deep networks are often overconfident; for a blend that sums probabilities, this matters directly.

Refresh. Train daily on a rolling window with warm starts, and run an incremental job more often for fast-moving signals; trending topics and news shift within hours, and a stale ranker degrades noticeably. Hold out the most recent day for validation.

Offline wins do not guarantee online wins. The logs only contain posts the old ranker chose to show, and AUC improvements on easy negatives can be meaningless. Treat offline metrics as a filter for what deserves an A/B test, not as proof.

Interviewers like a pitfall you have experienced. Mention A/B tests on social products suffer from network effects: if I am in treatment and my friend is in control, my better-ranked posts reach my friend, and my friend's reactions reach me. A plain user-level split then underestimates (or mixes up) the effect. Use graph-cluster randomization (Ugander et al.; Saveski et al.), measure on both viewers and creators, and run a long-term holdout.

Other A/B pitfalls to name: novelty effects (a new ordering raises engagement for a week), metric dilution from users who never saw the change, multiple-comparison inflation when many weight vectors are tested, and ramp-up bias when the early traffic is not representative.

Serving

Request path and scale math

At a high level the request flows through three stages: retrieve a few thousand eligible posts, score them with the heavy model, then apply list-level rules. The same funnel is used by Meta, which describes a lightweight pass to keep roughly 500 candidates, a main scoring pass, and a contextual pass for diversity.

Assumptions: 500 million daily users, 8 feed sessions each, so 4 billion requests per day, or 4e9 / 86,400 = about 46,000 requests per second on average. With a peak of 3x, that is about 140,000 QPS. Scoring 500 posts per request means 70 million post scores per second at peak. If one score costs about 10 MFLOPs, that is 7e14 FLOP/s, which is tens of accelerators or thousands of CPU cores before caching and prefetching reduce the load. At a 10 TB per day impression log, 30 days of history is 300 TB, which is why training reads a sampled stream.

Candidate retrieval

Retrieval collects every eligible post and caps each source so a prolific friend cannot flood the list. Two bumping rules matter: unread bumping re-admits posts that were ranked in an earlier session but never seen, and comment bumping re-admits already-seen posts that triggered a lively conversation among the viewer's connections.

For cold-start users with no friends yet, the candidate set shifts toward popular and trending posts in their region and topics from onboarding; modern systems may add an embedding-based retriever (LinkedIn describes replacing several separate retrieval sources with one LLM-embedding retriever) to surface posts from authors the viewer does not follow.

Architecture

The aggregator is the orchestrator: it calls retrieval, fans scoring requests out to many predictor machines in parallel, and passes the scored list to re-ranking. The feature store has two parts. A slow-changing part (viewer embeddings, long-term affinity) is refreshed in batch; a fast part (post counters, recent reaction velocity, last-hour affinity) is updated by streaming jobs within seconds to minutes. Freshness matters because a post that has just gone viral should be scored with its current like rate, and a user who just liked three cooking posts should see more cooking.

Re-ranking

The model does not know about list-level policy, so a final pass applies it. Keeping these rules outside the model keeps them auditable and quick to change.

Integrity and misinformation. Classifier scores (from the harmful-content system) enter in two places. Clearly violating posts are removed before ranking. Borderline content (misinformation likelihood, clickbait, engagement bait) is demoted by a multiplier on the blended value or by a feature the model sees, and labeled where appropriate, for example with a fact-check notice. The hide and report heads act as a second line: if users keep hiding a type of post, its blended value drops without a rule change.

Caching, prefetch, and degradation

  • Cache and prefetch. Cache the next page or two of the ranked list for a short time (minutes). Prefetch the first page for heavy users when they are likely to open the app, and refresh the cache when a close friend posts.
  • Fallbacks. If the ranking service times out, serve the cached list; if there is no cache, serve a reverse-chronological feed from retrieval with light affinity ordering. If the feature store is slow, score with defaults for missing features and mark the request so monitoring can see the impact.
  • Model rollout. Shadow-score new models on live traffic, compare score distributions and calibration to the incumbent, then ramp through A/B arms.
  • Refresh cadence. Full retraining daily; calibration layer refit more often; streaming features continuously.

Monitoring

  • Calibration drift: predicted versus observed rate per head per day; alert if the ratio leaves a band (for example 0.9 to 1.1).
  • Score distributions: mean and quantiles of each head, and of the blended value, per model version.
  • Feature freshness and coverage: age of streaming counters, share of requests with missing affinity features.
  • Ecosystem health: hides, reports, exposure by creator size, and share of impressions from non-friends.

Other Talking Points

  • Selection and position bias in training data: logged reactions only exist for posts that were shown, and top slots get more; use position as a training-only input, an exploration slice, or inverse-propensity weights (Joachims et al.), because otherwise the model learns the old ranker's mistakes.
  • Sequential models over interaction history: a transformer reading hundreds of past interactions captures taste trajectory and helps sparse users, but it needs GPU serving and shared-context batching (as LinkedIn describes), so propose it as an upgrade with a cost analysis.
  • Exploration: reserve a small share of slots for uncertain posts or authors (for example Thompson sampling over reaction rates) so new content can earn data; without it, the ranker only ever confirms what it already knows.
  • Long-term value versus short-term engagement: time spent can rise while satisfaction falls, so run long-running holdouts, survey-based quality metrics, and retention as the final judge of weight vectors.
  • Choosing blend weights: start from survey-informed priors, test candidate vectors as parallel arms, and keep guardrails fixed; the weights are a product decision as much as a modeling one, and they can drift as user behavior changes.
  • Integrity and misinformation demotion: keep classifier scores as both features and re-ranking inputs; demotion strength should be tunable without retraining, and appeals and audits should be possible.
  • Creator ecosystem health: track exposure by creator size and let small authors earn data; LinkedIn states it audits that creators compete on equal footing, which is a good guardrail to mention.
  • Explainability: "why am I seeing this?" is a product feature and a debugging tool; surface the top affinity and reaction drivers (for example "you often react to posts by this friend").
  • Training-serving skew: log the exact features used at request time and compute training features point-in-time, since counters and affinities change quickly.
  • Experimentation with interference: use graph-cluster randomization, creator-side metrics, and holdouts, because a user-level split leaks across friends.

Scoring 500 posts per request is 70M post scores per second; at an assumed 10 MFLOPs each that is 7e14 FLOP/s. Reduce it with the light pass 0 (score 1,500 cheaply, keep 500), batching, feature caching, a cached next page, prefetch for heavy users, and smaller models for low-engagement segments.
The weights in the blend encode product values. Who sets them, and how do you tune them without gaming the metric?

Keep roles separate. The model answers what the user will do (calibrated heads); the weights state what the company values. Set them through a process: start from a policy (what long-term outcomes matter: meaningful interactions, time well spent, integrity), then tune with online experiments using Bayesian optimization over the weights, with guardrails (hides, reports, retention) as constraints and the long-term metric as the objective.

To avoid Goodhart effects: version the weights, review them on a schedule with stakeholders (product, policy, integrity), keep a long-term holdout, and check for gaming by creators (engagement bait). Record each change with its experiment results so that value judgments are auditable, not hidden in code.

Likely follow-ups: How do you detect that creators are gaming a high-weight reaction? · What if two stakeholders want opposite changes?

What makes this Staff-level: You treat weights as governed product decisions with evidence, not a hand-tuned magic number.

Misinformation and borderline content get high predicted engagement. Where in the stack do you intervene?

At several layers, each for a reason. As a feature and label source: integrity classifier scores feed the ranker so it can learn the long-term cost. As a score modifier at re-rank time: a demotion multiplier for content flagged by classifiers or fact-checkers, which is cheaper to adjust than retraining. As a candidate filter: remove clearly violating items. As a product intervention: labels and friction for sharing.

Measure with integrity-specific metrics: prevalence of violating content views (sampled and human-labeled), distribution of exposure to flagged content, and the engagement cost of the demotions. Make the trade-off explicit: demotion strength is chosen from the experiment frontier of integrity gain against engagement loss and signed off by the owners.

Likely follow-ups: Why not just remove all borderline content? · How do you keep the demotion from becoming a hidden censorship system?

What makes this Staff-level: You apply different mechanisms at different layers and measure prevalence, not just removals.

You must cut serving cost by 30 percent without losing engagement. What is your plan?

Profile first: which stage, which model, which features cost the most per request, and how engagement relates to compute (diminishing returns). Candidate levers: a cascade with a light first-pass ranker (score 1,500 cheaply, keep 500 for the heavy model), caching user and item features and the next page, quantization (bf16 or int8) and distillation of the heavy ranker, early-exit or truncated models, and adaptive compute that spends more on high-value requests and less on low-value ones.

Evaluate each lever as a point on a cost-per-engagement curve with an A/B test, ship the ones that give the most savings per unit of lost engagement, and stop at the 30 percent target. Track a standing efficiency metric (cost per thousand sessions) so savings persist, and keep headroom for new features.

Likely follow-ups: Which lever has the highest risk of silent quality loss? · How do you decide which requests get more compute?

What makes this Principal-level: You treat cost as a first-class objective with a frontier and a sequence of measured steps.

How fresh must reaction features be, and how do you build near-real-time features correctly?

Decide by value: measure how much model quality decays as feature age grows (for example, retrain with features lagged by 1 minute, 10 minutes, 1 hour) and set a freshness SLO where the curve flattens. Build streaming counters (a windowed aggregation over an event stream) for the fast features and batch jobs for the slow ones; serve both from a feature store with the same definitions.

Correctness matters more than speed: log the features exactly as served and train on those logs (point-in-time correct), so training sees what serving saw. Monitor freshness (lag) and null rates per feature, and define a fallback (a default or a last-known value) with an alarm when a stream stalls.

Likely follow-ups: How do you backfill a new streaming feature for training? · What breaks if the stream lags by ten minutes during a viral event?

What makes this Staff-level: You justify the freshness tier with data and protect train-serve consistency.
