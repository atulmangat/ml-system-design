Chapter 7

Event Recommendation System

An Eventbrite or Meetup style platform earns money when people register for events, but an event happens once, locally, and usually with a handful of friends. By the time you have enough clicks to know an event is good, it is over. The core idea of this chapter: rank with content, place, time, and social features instead of collaborative history, and treat every new event as a cold-start item that must earn its first impressions through a controlled exploration budget.

Clarifying Requirements

Candidate

What is the product surface and the business goal?

Interviewer

A personalized "Events for you" feed on the home page, plus a weekly email and occasional push. The goal is more registrations and ticket sales.

Candidate

How big is the platform?

Interviewer

About 10 million users, 3 million daily active, and roughly 1 million active events (upcoming and not canceled) at any time.

Candidate

Are events in person, online, or both?

Interviewer

Mostly in person, so location is central. Online events exist; treat them with a flag so distance does not apply.

Candidate

What feedback signals do we log?

Interviewer

Impressions, clicks, bookmarks, and registrations (free RSVPs and paid tickets). Paid events also have a check-in scan; free events do not.

Candidate

Do we have a social graph?

Interviewer

Yes: friendships, invitations, and group memberships. Friend attendance can be shown only for friends who allow it.

Candidate

How quickly must a new event be recommendable, and how long do events live?

Interviewer

Within minutes of publishing. Most events are created two to six weeks ahead; many hosts run one event ever.

Candidate

What are the latency and freshness expectations?

Interviewer

The feed API should answer in under 200 ms at p99, and ticket counts or friend attendance should be no more than a minute stale.

Candidate

Can events be canceled, rescheduled, or sell out, and are they recurring?

Interviewer

All three happen. Treat every occurrence as its own event, and describe how the system reacts to state changes.

Candidate

Constraints and out of scope?

Interviewer

Privacy of social signals matters. Search, dynamic pricing, and recommending audiences to hosts are out of scope.

Design a personalized event feed for a platform with about 10M users, 3M daily actives, and 1M active events. The model predicts registrations from impressions, clicks, bookmarks, and RSVPs plus friend, location, and time context. Events are short-lived and local, so most are cold; the feed must rank within 200 ms and react to sold-out, canceled, and rescheduled events.

Events behave differently from videos or movies, and the whole design follows from that. A video accumulates views for years. An event collects most of its registrations in a short burst right before it starts, and then its label is closed forever. Macedo and Marinho analyzed Meetup data from three US metro areas: more than 45% of events had at most one positive RSVP, about 90% had ten or fewer, and positive RSVPs clustered close to the event date. Only about 30% of users had co-attended two or more events, an empirical ceiling for collaborative filtering.

An event is a perishable item: its useful life is weeks, its demand arrives late, and its capacity is finite. A model that waits for interaction history will always be one step behind, so features known at publish time (content, host, place, time) and social context carry the load.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

Several objectives sound plausible. The table compares them; the choice affects labels, calibration, and how you can later tune business rules.

Objective options
ObjectiveProsCons
Maximize clicks, P(click)Dense labels (about 4% of impressions); fast feedbackRewards curiosity and clickbait; weak link to tickets sold
Maximize registrations, P(register)Aligned with the business goal; works for free and paid eventsSparse (about 0.4% of impressions); labels arrive days later
Maximize expected revenue, P(register) times priceDirectly ties to ticket salesFavors expensive events; ignores free events that build future paid demand
Maximize attended registrations, P(register) times P(attend)Accounts for no-shows and host experienceAttendance is only observed for scanned tickets, and only after the event
Multi-task: heads for click, bookmark, registerDense signals regularize the sparse one; weights tunable laterNeeds calibrated heads and a tuned combination formula

State the decision with its reason: train a calibrated P(register) as the primary head, with click and bookmark as auxiliary heads that share representations. Apply price, no-show, and capacity effects afterwards in re-ranking, where they are easy to adjust, instead of baking them into labels.

Input and output

The input is a user, the request context, and a set of candidate events; the output is a probability per event that becomes a ranked page. Scoring all 1M events per request is unnecessary, because a user can only attend nearby, upcoming events.

ML category

This is a ranking problem with sparse implicit feedback. Compare the model families first.

Model families for event ranking
FamilyIdeaTrade-offs
Collaborative filtering (matrix factorization, KNN)Learn from who attended whatNeeds history per event; in the Meetup study KNN beat matrix factorization, and both lost much of their strength on cold events
Content and location onlyMatch categories, text, distance, timeWorks at publish time; ignores taste nuance and social pull
Pointwise learning to rankPredict P(register) for each (user, event) pair, then sortSimple, scalable, calibrated; ignores list context
Pairwise (RankNet, BPR)Learn that a registered event beats a skipped oneOptimizes order; pair count explodes and scores are not probabilities

Decision. Start with a pointwise ranker trained with log loss. It gives calibrated probabilities that you reuse for capacity throttling and revenue weighting. Run LambdaMART as an offline and online challenger once the pipeline is stable.

Distance and time shape behavior. Macedo and Marinho found that around half of positive RSVPs came from within 10 km, and almost none beyond 100 km. Time behaves differently by event type: free meetups fill in the last few days, while concerts and festivals sell over weeks. A tree model can learn these shapes from raw values; a linear or neural model benefits from bucketed distance and days-left plus crosses with category.

Smoothing early popularity. A new event shows 1 registration in 100 impressions, a raw rate of 1%. Use a category prior of 0.4% with strength 250 impressions: smoothed rate = (1 + 250 × 0.004) / (100 + 250) = 2 / 350 ≈ 0.57%. The estimate moves toward the prior until the event has enough data to speak for itself.

Early-popularity leakage. If you compute “registrations so far” at training time from the final table, you include the very registration you are trying to predict, plus all later ones. Build every popularity and friends-going feature as-of the impression timestamp, or simply use the feature values logged at serving time. Offline AUC that looks too good after adding popularity features is usually this bug.

Friend features and privacy. Use only friends whose RSVPs are visible, aggregate to counts, and never show or encode a specific hidden attendee. Treat sensitive categories (health, religion, politics, sexual orientation) as protected: do not infer sensitive traits from event history, and give users a way to hide events and turn off social signals.

Labels

Label sources
SourceMeaningStrength and flaw
Registration (RSVP or ticket)Strong intent, directly tied to revenueRare (about 0.4% of impressions); arrives late, often days after the impression
Click or bookmarkInterest signalDense, but clickbait-prone; bookmarks signal later intent
Check-in or ticket scanActually attendedMost reliable, but only for ticketed events and only after the event

Decision. The positive label for the primary head is a registration by the same user for that event within 7 days of an impression and before the event starts. Clicks and bookmarks train the auxiliary heads. A negative is an impression with no registration in the window. Collapse repeated impressions of the same (user, event) on the same day, and attribute the label to the last impression before the action.

Label sparsity. Assume 60M impressions and a 0.4% registration rate per day: 240K registrations. A 30-day window has 1.8B impressions and 1.8B × 0.004 = 7.2M positives. Per event, 60M / 1M = 60 impressions per day. A new event with 100 impressions has expected 0.4 registrations, and P(no registration) = 0.996100 ≈ 0.67. To see at least one registration with 95% probability you need n with 0.996n ≤ 0.05, so n ≈ ln(0.05) / ln(0.996) ≈ 747 impressions. Per-event conversion estimates are mostly noise for days, which is why priors matter.

Negative downsampling and recalibration. Keep every positive and a fraction w = 0.05 of negatives. Negatives: 1.8B − 7.2M = 1.7928B, times 0.05 = 89.64M. Rows after sampling: 7.2M + 89.64M = 96.84M, so the positive rate becomes 7.2 / 96.84 ≈ 7.4%. A model trained on this data predicts q on the sampled distribution, but real traffic has far more negatives. Because sampling multiplies the odds by 1/w, the true probability is p = q / (q + (1 − q) / w). Check: q = 0.0743 gives p = 0.0743 / (0.0743 + 0.9257 × 20) = 0.0743 / 18.588 ≈ 0.004, the base rate. A prediction of q = 0.30 becomes p = 0.30 / (0.30 + 0.70 × 20) = 0.30 / 14.3 ≈ 2.1%.

Other label problems. Delay: a registration can land days after the impression, so a nightly job re-joins labels and flips earlier negatives to positives (see Chapelle on delayed feedback for a modeling alternative). Position bias: top slots get more clicks regardless of quality, so log position, include it in training, and fix it to a neutral value at serving. Selection bias: only shown events have labels, so reserve a small slice of traffic (for example 1%) for randomized or exploratory impressions with logged propensities.

Model Development

Model options
ModelProsCons
Logistic regression with crossesFast, interpretable baseline; easy to calibrateManual feature crosses; weak on interactions such as distance by category
Gradient-boosted trees (pointwise or LambdaMART)Excellent on tabular features and missing values; quick to train and serve; strong cold-start behavior with content featuresWeak with raw text and high-cardinality IDs unless pre-embedded
GBDT leaves into logistic regressionUsed successfully in ads; combines trees' crosses with online-updatable weightsTwo-stage pipeline to maintain
Multi-task DNN with embeddingsLearns from text, IDs, and sequences; shares signal across click, bookmark, registerMore data and tuning; slower to debug; calibration needs care

Decision. Phase one: a GBDT with a pointwise log-loss objective, trained on downsampled data and recalibrated. Phase two: a multi-task DNN that consumes text and category embeddings, blended with or replacing the GBDT where offline and online metrics justify it. In the illustrative comparison below, the tree model captures most of the gain, and the DNN helps most on cold events through content embeddings.

Making the model survive cold start. DropoutNet (Volkovs et al.) randomly removes the collaborative inputs during training so the network learns to rely on content when they are missing. The same idea works for trees: set the early-popularity and per-event count features to missing for about 30% of training rows, and always for rows with fewer than 20 impressions. Without this, the model learns to lean on popularity and then misranks the new events it will see most of the time in production. Meta-learning approaches (Vartak et al.) are an alternative that adapt a per-user decision rule from item features, but they add training complexity that rarely pays off on top of a good content model.

Loss function

The primary loss is binary cross-entropy on P(register). Multi-task training adds auxiliary losses with weights that are tunable hyperparameters, for example L = Lreg + 0.5 Lbookmark + 0.2 Lclick (illustrative weights).

Log loss example. Loss for one example is −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)]. A registered example predicted at p = 0.02 costs −ln 0.02 = 3.91. A non-registered example predicted at p = 0.004 costs −ln 0.996 = 0.004. Rare positives dominate the loss, so a few missed registrations are expensive and the model is pushed to separate them.

LambdaMART intuition. Gradients are scaled by how much a swap changes nDCG. A registered event at rank 5 contributes 1 / log26 = 0.387. Moving it to rank 1 adds 1 − 0.387 = 0.613 to DCG; moving it to rank 4 adds 1 / log25 − 0.387 = 0.431 − 0.387 = 0.044. The listwise gradient pushes about 14 times harder on the first fix.

Training data construction and splitting

Split by time, not at random. A random split puts the same event, host, and friend neighborhood in both train and test, so the model memorizes event identity and the offline score overstates production quality. Leave a gap equal to the label window so training never sees labels that would not exist yet in production, and test mainly on events that were new in the test period.

Be explicit about why not fully online learning: feedback loops and noisy labels can drift the model, rollbacks are hard, and a bad update affects live traffic immediately. Fresh features plus daily retraining plus an hourly calibration step gives most of the benefit with far less risk.

Slice every metric by cold versus warm events, city size, free versus paid, and days until start. In the Meetup data, NDCG@20 stayed below about 0.3 even for the best simple recommenders, so expect low absolute numbers and compare relative lift to a popular-nearby baseline. Also keep in mind that logged data only contains shown events; where possible, use the randomized exploration slice or inverse-propensity weighting to check that a gain is not just agreement with the old ranker.

Sample size. Take registrations per session as the metric: 10 viewed impressions at 0.4% gives 1 − 0.99610 ≈ 0.04 per session, so variance ≈ 0.04 × 0.96 = 0.0384. To detect a 2% relative lift (0.0008 absolute) with 80% power at 5% significance, n per arm ≈ 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)2 × 0.0384 / 0.00082 = 15.68 × 0.0384 / 6.4×10-7 ≈ 0.94M sessions. At 6M sessions per day that is under a day on paper, but the real test must still run two to three weeks.

Two-sided interference. Treatment and control users compete for the same limited tickets. If the new model sells out an event for treatment users, control users lose seats, so the measured lift is partly stolen demand and overstates the platform gain. Friends in different arms also share social signals, and events live for weeks, so one-day tests miss the lifecycle. Randomize by geographic cluster (for example H3 cells at a coarse resolution plus a buffer, or whole metros), run for at least two to three weeks to cover registration timing, compare against a holdout, and track capacity guardrails such as sold-out exposure and refunds. Cluster designs have far fewer independent units, so plan for lower power.

Serving

Candidate generation

Scoring 1M events per request is wasteful. A user in a big metro has a few thousand plausible events inside a 25 km radius (assumed density of about 2 per km2), and most of them are irrelevant for a given session. Use several cheap sources, merge, and send about 500 events to the ranker.

Geo-index sizing. Index events in a hierarchical grid such as H3 or S2 (or geohash). At H3 resolution 6 a hexagon averages 36.1 km2 with an edge of 3.7 km; at resolution 7 it is 5.16 km2 with a 1.4 km edge; at resolution 5 it is 252.9 km2. A 25 km radius covers π × 252 ≈ 1,963 km2, which is 1,963 / 36.1 ≈ 54 resolution-6 cells, or 61 cells for a k-ring with k = 4 (3k(k+1) + 1 = 61, about 2,200 km2). That is 61 posting-list lookups, versus roughly 380 at resolution 7. Fetch cells, then apply an exact distance filter. Keep posting lists keyed by cell and start day so time filters are cheap, and remove events when they start, sell out, or cancel.

Ranking and re-ranking

The ranker scores the 500 candidates with fresh features. Re-ranking then applies product rules that a pure probability cannot express.

Capacity-aware throttling. An event has 20 tickets left and the model predicts p = 2% per impression. Expected impressions to fill it = 20 / 0.02 = 1,000. Allow roughly 1.5 times that, about 1,500 impressions, then stop promoting it. Showing it to 10,000 users would burn 9,000 slots on an event that will say “sold out”. Add expected no-shows for paid tickets only after you measure them: with a 25% no-show rate (assumption) 20 seats could be sold to about 20 / 0.75 ≈ 27 buyers if the host allows overbooking, otherwise do not.

Serving architecture

Latency and scale (assumptions). 3M daily actives with 2 sessions each gives 6M feed requests per day, about 70 per second on average and about 550 per second at an 8x peak. Scoring 500 candidates per request is 550 × 500 = 275K scorings per second. At roughly 100 microseconds per scoring for a 300-tree GBDT, that is about 28 cores, so a small fleet with headroom handles it. A plausible budget: candidate lookup 30 ms, feature fetch 20 ms, ranking 40 ms, re-ranking 10 ms, network and serialization 40 ms, leaving margin under 200 ms. Active events at about 2 KB of features each take 2 GB, and 10M users at 1 KB take 10 GB, so the online feature store fits in memory.

Monitoring. Watch calibration (predicted versus observed registration rate per bucket and per city, hourly), feature drift (population stability for distance, days-left, and counts), stream lag, the share of impressions going to sold-out or canceled events, share of new events getting first impressions within an hour, and slice metrics by city size.

Cold-start strategy for new events

A new event gets a prior from content, host, place, and time, then earns evidence through a limited exploration budget, and finally graduates to the warm ranker. The exploration budget needs arithmetic: if one position in 10 is reserved, that is 6M impressions per day. With about 33K new events per day (1M active over a 30-day life) each gets about 180 exploratory impressions on average.

Bayesian update. Model the event's registration rate as Beta(1, 249), mean 1/250 = 0.4%. After 180 exploratory impressions: with 0 registrations the posterior mean is 1 / 430 = 0.23%; with 2 registrations it is 3 / 430 = 0.70%. Even at 180 impressions, P(zero registrations) = 0.996180 ≈ 0.49, so half of average events still have no label; the prior and the content model decide their ranking until real evidence arrives.

Event lifecycle and notifications

Every state change must reach the index, the feature store, and registered users. Lifecycle events are first-class messages in the log.

Notification timing. Pushes and emails reuse the ranker with a stricter bar and a frequency cap (for example one push per day and three per week). Free meetups benefit from a nudge in the last 48 hours; paid concerts from an early message plus a reminder before the sale closes or stock runs low. Do not notify about an event with only a handful of tickets left, and measure uplift (who registers because of the message) rather than just opens, so the system does not spam users who would have come anyway.

Other Talking Points

  • Recurring events as series: model the series for taste and host quality, but score each occurrence with its own date, capacity, and counts, so a sold-out Friday does not hide an open Saturday.
  • Travelers and moved users: use current location when it is recent, otherwise home cell, because a stale home cell sends a visitor irrelevant suggestions.
  • Group recommendations: when a user plans with friends, rank for the group using the friends who opt in, since friend attendance is both a feature and a product surface.
  • Calendar integration: a conflict flag removes events the user cannot attend, which raises precision without touching the model.
  • Online events: treat distance as not applicable and add timezone handling, otherwise they either dominate or vanish from local feeds.
  • Spam and fraud: screen fake or scam events with an integrity model before ranking, because the ranker will happily promote an event that converts well and refunds badly.
  • Explanations: show reasons such as “two friends are going” or “near you”; they raise trust and click-to-register rate and give users a way to correct the system.
  • Seasonality and holidays: add calendar features and keep a seasonal baseline, because Valentine's weekend and festival season break last month's patterns.
  • Contextual bandits (for example LinUCB) for the exploration slot when the number of candidate new events is large and rewards are delayed.
  • Host-side value: report to organizers how much exposure they got and why, because supply growth depends on hosts believing the marketplace is fair.

Check yourself

Questions are tagged by the level they target. The first set checks Senior-level fundamentals and arithmetic; Staff and Principal questions probe trade-offs, operations, experimentation and organization. Try to answer aloud for two minutes before opening one.

For rough sizing, per-user randomization on registrations per session (variance about 0.0384, 2% relative lift) needs about 0.94M sessions per arm, so the cluster design may require a longer run.

Organizers want exposure, attendees want relevance, and tickets are limited. How do you formulate the objective?

Maximize expected registrations subject to capacity and fairness constraints. A capacity-aware score multiplies the predicted conversion by a factor that falls as remaining tickets approach what the pace of current demand will fill, so you stop showing an event that will sell out and give exposure to ones that need it. The impression budget for an event with 8 tickets left at 2 percent conversion is about 8 / 0.02 = 400 impressions.

Add organizer-side fairness: an exposure floor for new organizers so they can build history, and a cap so a single organizer cannot dominate. Guardrails: refund and cancellation rate, "sold out" disappointment, and organizer retention. Evaluate with marketplace-level experiments, for example randomizing by city, because user-level tests leak across the shared inventory.

Likely follow-ups: What is the failure when predicted conversion is miscalibrated? · How do you avoid showing sold-out events during the delay in inventory updates?

What makes this Staff-level: You see events as a constrained marketplace, not a pure ranking problem, and you choose the experiment unit to match.

You launch in a brand-new city with no history. What does the system do on day one?

Lean on features that transfer: content (category, title text embeddings), time and day patterns, price, host history from other cities, and distance expressed relative to the city's density rather than in absolute kilometers. Use priors from similar cities (clustered by category mix and size) for popularity and for calibration offsets. Add exploration so the first week generates data, and use social signals (friends' registrations, imported contacts) where available.

Evaluate from the start with a small, randomized exploration slice, and compare the new city's calibration curve to existing cities before trusting scores. Avoid high-capacity ID embeddings in the first weeks; they have no data. Measure time-to-reasonable-quality (days until calibration error falls within tolerance) as a launch KPI.

Likely follow-ups: How would you tell that the city-similarity prior is wrong? · What do you do about seasonality you have not seen in this city?

What makes this Staff-level: You plan for the cold start as a repeatable launch playbook with its own KPI.

The business wants push notifications to drive registrations. How do you measure true impact and avoid fatigue?

Optimize for incremental registrations, not click rate. Notifications often reach people who would have registered anyway, so a model that predicts click will target the wrong users. Estimate the treatment effect with a randomized holdout (users who would have been messaged but are not) and train an uplift model, or at least rank by predicted lift.

Control fatigue with per-user budgets, quiet hours, send-time optimization and frequency caps tuned by the opt-out curve. Guardrails: unsubscribe and notification-disable rate, uninstall rate, and long-term engagement of heavily messaged cohorts. Review quarterly: if a cohort's opt-outs rise, the notification program is borrowing from the future.

Likely follow-ups: How large must the holdout be and how long does it run? · What would make you turn the channel off for a user?

What makes this Principal-level: You distinguish correlation from causal lift and account for the long-term cost of the channel.

AUC is 0.82, but organizers in suburban areas report that their events get almost no exposure. Diagnose.

Check for density and popularity bias: features such as distance to city center, venue popularity and past registrations are correlated with location, so the model learns "downtown wins". Slice evaluation by region (impressions per event, calibration per region) before concluding anything; the aggregate AUC hides it. Check position bias and the candidate stage too: a fixed-radius candidate generator can starve suburban events.

Fixes: calibrate per region, add exposure floors and exploration for under-served areas, adjust candidate radius by density (the H3 resolution choice), and train with propensity weights from logged impressions. Report exposure Gini by region and organizer as a standing fairness metric.

Likely follow-ups: How do you separate low exposure from low demand? · What does an exposure floor cost in conversions?
