Chapter 5

Harmful Content Detection

A social platform receives about 100 million new posts a day and roughly one in a thousand breaks policy. The system must find those posts in any mix of text, image, and video across many languages, remove clear violations, demote borderline ones, and send the uncertain middle to human reviewers, all within seconds to minutes while adversaries keep changing tactics. The core idea is one calibrated multi-label model with a threshold ladder on top: probabilities pick the action, humans resolve the doubt, and every human decision becomes the next training label.

Clarifying Requirements

This problem is as much about policy and operations as about modeling. A strong candidate spends the first minutes pinning down what actions exist, what a mistake costs in each direction, and how much human capacity is available.

Candidate

What can the system do to a harmful post?

Interviewer

Three things: remove clear violations and tell the author why, demote borderline posts so fewer people see them, and send uncertain posts to human reviewers. Authors can appeal removals.

Candidate

What is in scope as input: posts only, or comments and messages too?

Interviewer

Posts only. A post may have text, an image, a video, or any mix. Comments and private messages are out of scope, but reports, hides, and flagged replies on a post can be used as signals.

Candidate

Which harm types, and can one post violate several?

Interviewer

About a dozen categories, such as violence, nudity, hate speech, self-harm, harassment, and spam. A post can violate several at once. Policy text changes a few times a year, and some rules differ by country.

Candidate

What scale should I design for?

Interviewer

Assume 100 million new posts per day, about 10% of them with video. Assume roughly 0.1% violate policy, so positives are rare.

Candidate

How fast must we act?

Interviewer

A decision for 95% of text and image posts within 30 seconds, and a few minutes for video. The earlier we act, the fewer people see the post, but speed must not cost accuracy on removals.

Candidate

What labels do we have?

Interviewer

Human reviewer decisions, user reports, and a small random sample that reviewers label for measurement. Reports are noisy and sometimes organized attacks on innocent posts.

Candidate

Which languages, and how good is the data in each?

Interviewer

More than 50 languages. A handful have abundant labels; many have only a few hundred positives.

Candidate

What do the two kinds of mistakes cost?

Interviewer

A wrong removal hurts trust and has legal exposure, so automatic removal needs at least 95% precision. A miss exposes people to harm, so recall matters too. Reviewers can handle about 150,000 items per day.

Candidate

Is anything personalized, and how should new accounts or new harm types be handled?

Interviewer

No personalization: the same policy applies to everyone, adjusted only by country. New accounts and brand-new harm types are the cold-start cases, so plan for them.

Candidate

What is out of scope?

Interviewer

Encrypted private messaging, account-level bans, and ads review. Known illegal imagery is handled by hash matching with a legal workflow, which you can mention in one line.

Build a proactive system that scores every new post (any mix of text, image, and video, in 50+ languages) for about a dozen harm types, then removes clear violations, demotes borderline ones, and routes uncertain ones to human reviewers. Scale is 100 million posts a day at roughly 0.1% prevalence, with decisions in under 30 seconds for most posts, at least 95% precision for automatic removal, and about 150,000 reviews a day of capacity. Users are told why and can appeal.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

The system must turn a post into a decision, but the model should not make the decision itself. The model's job is to estimate how likely the post is to violate each policy. The action then follows from thresholds that policy and operations teams can change without retraining.

Candidate objectives
OptionWhat the model predictsProsCons
Single binary classifier"Harmful or not"Simplest to build and evaluateCannot say which policy was broken, so no useful notice to the author; one threshold for very different harms
Multi-class softmaxExactly one of N harm types or "none"Familiar loss; outputs sum to 1Wrong assumption: a threatening, hateful meme is violence and hate at once; adding a policy changes every other probability

Multi-label, multi-taskIndependent calibrated probability per harm class from a shared backboneShared features, one forward pass, per-class thresholds, a natural explanationTasks can interfere; needs per-class evaluation and care with missing labels

Predict a calibrated probability per harm class and keep the action choice outside the model. Policies, country rules, and review capacity change faster than a model can be retrained, and a threshold is the cheapest knob to turn.

Input and output

The input is the whole post, not only its text: caption, text found inside images, sampled video frames, an audio transcript, and linked domains. Author and posting-context features come with it. After exposure, reaction signals such as reports and hides join in a second-pass model.

The output is a vector of probabilities, one per harm class, plus an explanation handle such as the text span or frame that drove the top score. Probabilities are independent, so a post can score high on several classes while a news clip about a war stays below every threshold.

ML category

The backbone choice is separate from the multi-label framing. A candidate may compare four families and say which fits each stage.

Label noise and agreement

Reviewers disagree most on exactly the posts the model is unsure about. Measure it with Cohen's kappa: the observed agreement minus the agreement expected by chance, divided by what is left over. Low kappa on a policy usually means the written guidance is ambiguous, and no model can beat that ceiling.

Joint shares from the matrix: observed agreement po = 0.3 + 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.60. Row totals for reviewer A are 0.4, 0.3, 0.3, and the column totals for B are the same, so chance agreement pe = 0.4×0.4 + 0.3×0.3 + 0.3×0.3 = 0.16 + 0.09 + 0.09 = 0.34. Kappa = (0.60 − 0.34) / (1 − 0.34) = 0.26 / 0.66 ≈ 0.39, only fair agreement even though 60% of the posts matched. Fix the guidance, add a second review for this policy, and use soft labels (the fraction of reviewers voting violating) instead of hard 0 or 1.

Imbalance, delay, and selection bias

At 0.1% prevalence a model that says "clean" for everything scores 99.9% accuracy. Keep every positive, sample the negatives, and compensate afterwards, as the next figure shows.

  • Delay. A final label can take hours, and an appeal can overturn it days later. Train on posts older than a maturity window (for example 7 days) and apply overturns as corrections.
  • Selection bias. Reviewers only see what reports or the current model surface, so a model trained on them never learns about harms it already misses. Keep a random labeled sample, and use inverse-propensity weights for the surfaced items.
  • Hard negatives. Include benign look-alikes: news about violence, medical nudity, quoted hate speech in a rebuttal, reclaimed slurs. Without them the model learns surface keywords.

Model Development

Choosing the architecture

Harm often lives in the interaction of modalities. The Hateful Memes challenge was built from "benign confounders", pairs where swapping the image or the caption flips the label, so unimodal models cannot cheat. Its authors reported 64.73% accuracy for the strongest methods then against 84.7% for humans, which is the gap a design must close.

Fusion strategies
StrategyHow it worksProsCons
EarlyConcatenate frozen text and image features, then an MLPCheap; easy to train and debugWeak interaction between modalities
LateOne classifier per modality; combine the scores with weights or a small tree modelModular; each modality can be missing; reuses existing modelsNo interaction at all: harmless image plus hateful caption looks fine to both
JointCross-modal transformer over text tokens and image regions (ViLBERT, VisualBERT, CLIP-based fusion)Learns meaning that exists only across modalities; best on memesCostliest to train and serve; needs paired data

Decision. Start with joint fusion built on pretrained encoders, with the cross-modal interaction kept shallow (one light layer): a multilingual text encoder (XLM-R style, 100 languages), a vision encoder (CLIP-style), and one light cross-attention fusion layer. This keeps the encoders reusable, captures meme-style interactions, and degrades gracefully when a modality is missing. Meta has described the same direction publicly: a single whole-post model trained across content types, replacing dozens of single-purpose classifiers.

Training data construction

A training set is built per harm class from three pools plus a protected holdout. Positives are all reviewed violations. Hard negatives are benign posts that scored high or were reported and then cleared. Random negatives give the model the true variety of ordinary content. The holdout is a random slice of the live stream that is never trained on and keeps the true 0.1% prior for evaluation and calibration.

With 100K positives, 100K hard negatives, and 999K random negatives the positive share is 100 / (100 + 100 + 999) ≈ 8.3%.

Loss function

Each head is trained with binary cross-entropy, summed over classes, with a mask for classes whose label is unknown (a post reviewed only for nudity says nothing about spam). Because easy negatives dominate even after sampling, focal loss down-weights examples the model already gets right: FL = −(1 − pt)γ log(pt), where pt is the probability assigned to the true label.

With γ = 2. An easy clean post scored 0.99 clean: cross-entropy is −ln 0.99 = 0.0101 and the modulating factor is (1 − 0.99)2 = 0.0001, so focal loss is about 0.000001. A hard violation the model gives only 0.1: cross-entropy is −ln 0.1 = 2.303 and the factor is 0.92 = 0.81, so focal loss is 1.865. The easy example's loss shrank 10,000-fold; the hard one barely changed, so gradients come from the mistakes.

Focal loss and class weights distort probabilities, so the raw output is a ranking score, not a probability. That is why calibration is a separate step.

Calibration and thresholds

Thresholds are only meaningful if 0.95 means "about 95% of posts scored like this truly violate". Fit a calibrator (Platt scaling, a logistic curve on the score, or isotonic regression, a monotone step function) on the unbiased holdout, per harm class and per language group, and report expected calibration error. Calibrating on the down-sampled training data would be wrong.

Down-sampling clean posts shifts the odds. If clean posts are kept at rate r, true odds = r × sampled odds. A model trained with r = 0.01 outputs 0.9 (sampled odds 9), so the true odds are 0.09 and the true probability is 0.09 / 1.09 ≈ 0.083, not 0.9. At r = 0.1, the same 0.9 means true odds 0.9 and probability 0.9 / 1.9 ≈ 0.47. A calibrator on the unbiased holdout absorbs this automatically.

Pick thresholds from costs. Let W be the cost of wrongly removing a benign post, R the cost of one human review (which resolves the item), and E the cost of one missed violation staying up, all in the same arbitrary units.

  • Remove automatically when removing is cheaper than reviewing: (1 − p) × W < R, so p > 1 − R/W.
  • Send to review when reviewing is cheaper than leaving the post up: p × E > R, so p > R/E.

Take R = 1, W = 20 (a wrongful removal costs twenty reviews), E = 2.5. Removal threshold: 1 − 1/20 = 0.95. Review threshold: 1/2.5 = 0.40. These are the cut points used throughout this chapter. If W falls to 10, the removal threshold drops to 0.90; if reviewers become scarce and R doubles to 2, the review threshold rises to 0.80 and the removal threshold to 0.90 with W = 20.

Multilingual coverage and memes

A multilingual encoder lets high-resource languages lend strength to low-resource ones, but transfer is uneven. Slang, code-switching ("Hinglish" typed in Latin script), and dialect all shift the distribution. Practical levers: oversample low-resource positives, translate labeled posts into other languages as augmentation, calibrate and set thresholds per language group, and recruit native-language reviewers for the review queues. Meta reports that its consolidated cross-problem classifiers reached many markets in weeks rather than months.

For memes, run OCR first and give the model both the extracted text and the pixels. OCR must survive stylized fonts, low contrast, and overlaid noise, so train it on augmented memes. The text becomes a normal text input, and the cross-attention layer decides whether the caption and the image combine into something harmful.

New harms and the learning loop

When a new harm appears (a viral conspiracy phrase, a fresh dangerous challenge), months of labeling is too slow. Few-shot approaches reformulate the task so the model reads a policy sentence plus a handful of examples. Meta's Few-Shot Learner uses entailment: a post is paired with a label description such as "this is hate speech" and the model decides whether the post entails it. The approach works with zero examples, under 50 demonstrations, or light fine-tuning, and Meta reports adaptation in weeks instead of months. Treat its outputs as review-band candidates until labels accumulate.

Labeling effort is the scarcest resource, so spend it wisely with active learning: fill part of each queue with uncertain items (scores near the review threshold), part with diverse items (cluster-sampled so reviewers do not see 500 copies of one meme), and part with a random slice that keeps estimates unbiased.

Why ROC-AUC misleads. At threshold 0.10 the false positive rate is 1%, which looks excellent on a ROC plot with recall at 0.97. But 1% of 99.9M clean posts is 999,000 wrongly flagged posts against 97,000 real catches: precision = 97,000 / (97,000 + 999,000) = 97,000 / 1,096,000 ≈ 8.9%. The area under the ROC curve is at least 0.97 × 0.99 = 0.960 from that one point alone, so ROC-AUC sits near 0.99 whether the model is good or mediocre. Precision-recall exposes the cost because precision divides by what was flagged, not by the huge clean population.

PR-AUC by trapezoids over the multimodal curve: 0.1995 + 0.1980 + 0.1463 + 0.1361 + 0.0750 + 0.0448 + 0.0173 + 0.0062 + 0.0017 ≈ 0.82. The text-only curve gives about 0.71. A 0.01 gap in ROC-AUC hides an eleven-point gap in average precision.

Across the whole system, removal catches 55,000 violations and the review band (scores from 0.40 up to 0.95) contributes 33,000 more if reviewers act in time, so combined recall is 88,000 / 100,000 = 88%. The band holds 33,000 true violations and 98,000 clean posts, so 131,000 items a day, with in-band precision of 33,000 / 131,000 ≈ 25%. That fits the 150,000-item capacity, and at an assumed 400 items per reviewer shift it needs about 330 shifts a day. About 2,000 posts a day are wrongly auto-removed; if 30% of those authors appeal (assumption), expect about 600 appeals a day.

Slices

A global metric hides the worst performance. Always slice by harm class, modality, language, country, and author group, and check that each slice has enough positives to be meaningful (a slice with 20 positives gives a precision interval of roughly plus or minus 20 points).

Evaluating on reviewer-surfaced data alone inflates every metric. Items were chosen because earlier systems or users already flagged them, so the evaluation set is easier than the real stream. Measure recall and prevalence on a random sample labeled independently of the model.

Fairness and explainability checks

Hate-speech models can penalize dialect: one study found tweets written in African American English up to two times more likely to be labeled offensive, partly because annotators were insensitive to dialect. Evaluate false positive rate across dialect and identity-term slices (including reclaimed terms and quoted slurs), use counterfactual augmentation that swaps identity terms, and prime annotators with dialect context. Report the false positive rate gap between slices as a guardrail.

Online metrics

The headline metric is prevalence: the share of all content views that were of violating content, estimated by labeling a random sample of views. It measures what people experienced after all enforcement, so it falls when removal is both accurate and early. Meta publishes it that way, uses stratified sampling to cut variance, has two reviewers label sampled items with a third as tiebreaker, and reports an upper bound when too few violations are found.

Sample size. At prevalence p = 0.1% the 95% interval half-width is 1.96 × √(p(1 − p)/n). With n = 100,000 labeled views it is 1.96 × √(0.000999 / 100,000) ≈ 0.0196 percentage points, so 0.10% ± 0.02. To reach ± 0.01 points (10% relative) you need n = 1.962 × 0.000999 / 0.00012 ≈ 384,000 labeled views. If zero violating views appear in n = 100,000, the 95% upper bound is about 3 / n = 0.003%.

A/B pitfalls. Content spreads between users, so randomizing viewers leaks the treatment (a post removed for the test group is still seen by the control group). Prevalence needs very large samples, so run tests long enough, and expect adversaries to adapt a few weeks after any launch, which makes early gains look larger than they last.

Serving

Architecture

Scoring is asynchronous and event-driven: nothing blocks the user's post. A cheap first pass handles everything, and a second pass fuses reactions as they appear.

Step by step:

  1. A post event lands on the stream. A hash and rule filter catches known violating media (perceptual hashes such as PDQ) and obvious patterns in milliseconds, with no model.
  2. The first-pass scorer, a distilled multimodal model, writes per-class probabilities. Only posts above a low cascade threshold (assume 5%) go on to the heavier ensemble.
  3. The policy engine applies the ladder and country rules: remove, demote, enqueue for review, or allow. Removal triggers the user notice with the policy named.
  4. Reports, hides, and flagged replies stream into the aggregator, which weights reporters by trust. The second-pass scorer reruns on posts whose reaction counts cross a trigger and may escalate.
  5. Review decisions enter the label store, which feeds retraining, recalibration, and the next deployment.

Review queue prioritization

Reviewers are the bottleneck, so order the queue by expected harm, not by arrival time: P(violating) times severity of the harm times predicted reach (views the post will receive in the next hours).

Add a time-in-queue boost so low-priority items are not starved, and a hard ceiling for severe categories such as threats or self-harm, which jump the queue. If a queue overflows, items already demoted stay demoted and expire to a decision rule rather than being published unreviewed.

Latency and scale

Assumptions: 100 million posts a day is 100,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 1,157 posts per second on average and about 3,500 per second at 3x peak. Text appears on 90% of posts, 35% carry images, and 10% carry video. Throughput per GPU for small distilled models is 800 text items per second, 500 images per second, 200 OCR images per second, and 40 videos per second (8 frames plus audio).

Failure modes, refresh, and monitoring

  • Scorer backlog or outage. Publish with a reach cap for high-risk surfaces (new accounts, high-severity categories) until scored. Do not fail fully open or fully closed, and process the backlog by priority.
  • Reviewer shortage. Demotions stay in force, the queue is ordered by expected harm, and the review threshold rises temporarily with an alert.
  • Bad model release. Roll out by shadow, then 1%, 10%, 100% with automatic rollback on appeal overturn spikes or queue yield drops.
  • Refresh cadence. Hashes and rules in hours, thresholds and calibration daily on a fresh holdout, the model weekly or on drift, few-shot adapters in days for new harms.
  • Monitoring. Score distribution drift per class and language, calibration on the weekly random sample, precision of auto-removals via an audit re-review of about 1% of removals, queue age, time to action, and slice false positive rates.

Appeals and user notice

Users get the policy category, an excerpt or region that triggered it, and a one-click appeal. Do not reveal thresholds or feature details, which gives attackers a map. A different reviewer handles each appeal. Overturns are the cleanest false-positive label available and flow back as hard negatives.

Other Talking Points

  • Adversarial evasion (character swaps, homoglyphs, text in images, cropped or mirrored video): normalize text, run OCR, use near-duplicate matching to known violators, and train on adversarial augmentations, because attackers adapt within weeks of any deployment.
  • Graph and account-level signals: coordinated networks reveal themselves through shared posting patterns even when each post looks benign, so add a network detector next to the content model.
  • Context-dependent policy: news reporting, satire, and condemnation use the same words as violations, so give the model author, group, and reply context and measure hard-negative slices separately.
  • Explainability: show the policy category and the triggering region, because a vague "this violated our rules" drives appeals and distrust, but withhold thresholds to avoid teaching evasion.
  • Privacy and reviewer wellbeing: minimize who sees raw content, blur by default, cap exposure, and keep model-first triage so the worst material reaches fewer humans.
  • Calibration drift: every retrain shifts the score distribution, so refit calibrators and thresholds on a fresh holdout before each release or a threshold of 0.95 will silently mean something else.
  • Policy versioning: keep policy text, label guidelines, model version, and thresholds linked, so you can reconstruct why a decision was made and re-label history when policy changes.
  • Few-shot and LLM assistance: use them to bootstrap new harms and speed up labeling, but never as the sole basis of removal, because they are uncalibrated and expensive.
  • Regional rules: laws differ by country, so the policy engine applies country overlays outside the model, which keeps one global model and avoids retraining per jurisdiction.
  • Known illegal imagery: match perceptual hashes against curated databases with a legal reporting path, because exact policy compliance matters more than model accuracy for that category.

Check yourself

Questions are tagged by the level they target. The first set checks Senior-level fundamentals and arithmetic; Staff and Principal questions probe trade-offs, operations, experimentation and organization. Try to answer aloud for two minutes before opening one.

A colleague reports ROC-AUC 0.99 and says the model is ready. Prevalence is 0.1% and the system flags at a 1% false positive rate with 97% recall. What do you reply?For 100M posts a day there are 100,000 violations and 99.9M clean posts. A 1% false positive rate is 999,000 wrongly flagged posts, against 97,000 true catches, so precision is 97,000 / 1,096,000 ≈ 8.9%. Over 90% of removals or review items would be wrong. Report PR-AUC and recall at 95% precision, and choose the threshold from the precision-recall curve, not from ROC.
Derive the auto-removal threshold when a wrongful removal is 20 times as costly as one human review. What happens if reviewers get twice as expensive?Removing is better than reviewing when (1 − p) × W < R, so p > 1 − R/W = 1 − 1/20 = 0.95. If R doubles to 2 while W stays 20, the threshold is 1 − 2/20 = 0.90, so more posts are removed automatically because review is dearer. The calibrated probability is what makes these thresholds meaningful, which is why calibration happens on an unbiased holdout.
You trained with 1% of clean posts and the model outputs 0.9 for a post. What is the true probability? What if you had kept 10%?True odds = keep rate × sampled odds. At 1%: sampled odds 9, true odds 0.09, probability 0.09 / 1.09 ≈ 0.083. At 10%: true odds 0.9, probability 0.9 / 1.9 ≈ 0.47. Either way the raw score is inflated. Fit a calibrator on a random holdout with the true prior and apply it before comparing to thresholds.
A post gets 5,000 reports in ten minutes. Should it be removed automatically?No. Report volume can be an organized attack to silence a victim or a rival, and it is only a second-pass feature. Weight each report by the reporter's historical accuracy, look for correlated reporters (same group, new accounts), and check how the rate compares with views. Escalate the post to the review queue with a temporary demotion, and let a human decide. Auto-removal needs the model's calibrated score above 0.95 independent of report counts, or a trusted-reporter threshold that has itself been validated.
Burmese has 300 labeled positives and recall at 95% precision of 0.25. What do you do?First confirm the slice is measured on enough data (300 positives gives a wide interval), then raise coverage in layers: oversample and translate positives from other languages, use multilingual pretraining and few-shot adaptation, hire native-language reviewers and focus active learning there, and calibrate and threshold the language separately. Until recall improves, route more Burmese content into review rather than raising the removal threshold, and report the gap openly in dashboards.
How many labeled views do you need to estimate prevalence of 0.1% to within plus or minus 0.01 percentage points?n = 1.962 × p(1 − p) / d2 = 3.8416 × 0.000999 / 0.00012 ≈ 384,000 labeled views at 95% confidence. Stratified sampling that oversamples surfaces where violations are more likely reduces this, as does labeling in two stages, but the order of magnitude explains why prevalence needs an organized, funded sampling program rather than ad hoc labels.
A new scam format spreads on Friday and your model has never seen it. Walk through the first 72 hours.

Hours 0 to 6: detect and size the problem from report spikes and embedding-cluster anomalies, and get the policy team to define the violation precisely. Contain: add exact and near-duplicate hash matching, text patterns, and a nearest-neighbor lookup against a small seed set embedded with the frozen backbone, with conservative actions (demote and queue for review, not auto-remove). Hours 6 to 24: stand up labeling, sample the flagged content to estimate precision, and tune thresholds so the review queue is manageable.

Hours 24 to 72: train a lightweight head on the first one to two thousand labels using the frozen backbone, check calibration and recall on a fresh sample, and promote the temporary rules into the model. Give every temporary rule an expiry and an owner, and document the incident. Watch for over-removal of legitimate content (news reporting about the scam).

Likely follow-ups: How many labels do you need before a trained head beats the rules? · How do you prevent the temporary rules from becoming permanent?

What makes this Staff-level: You sequence containment, measurement and learning, and you protect against over-enforcement while the model catches up.

Adversaries change tactics weekly. How do you build robustness that outlasts whack-a-mole fixes?

Combine data, model and process. Data: augment training with the known evasions (homoglyphs, spacing, text-in-image, crops, mirrors, filters) and add a stream of fresh labels from reviewers, appeals and honeypot accounts. Model: use signals that are hard to change, such as account behavior and network structure, alongside content, and ensemble different views so one evasion does not defeat all of them. Process: a red-team that attacks each release, and an "escaped violations" audit: sample content the system passed, have humans label it, and track the escape rate as a first-class metric.

Also avoid leaking decision boundaries: do not expose exact thresholds or scores to users, and delay or randomize some enforcement so attackers cannot probe cheaply. Robustness is measured by how quickly escape rate recovers after a new tactic, not by a static test set.

Likely follow-ups: How do you estimate the escape rate when positives are 0.1 percent of traffic? · What does adversarial training cost you on clean data?

What makes this Staff-level: You measure what gets through, not just what you caught, and you attack the economics of evasion.

Two countries have conflicting legal standards for the same content. How do the architecture and the organization handle it?

Separate perception from policy. One global model predicts calibrated probabilities for each harm class; a policy layer maps probabilities and context (country, surface, age of viewer) to actions with locale-specific thresholds and remedies (geo-restrict, label, remove globally). That layer is versioned, audited and owned jointly with legal and policy teams, so a law change is a configuration change, not a model retrain.

Evaluate per locale with locale-native reviewers and per-locale labeled sets, because language and context differ. Log every action with the rule version for appeals and regulator requests. Organizationally, define who can change which part: policy owns definitions, ML owns models and calibration, legal approves region rules, and an escalation path handles conflicts.

Likely follow-ups: What if a region requires removal within an hour? · How do you keep the model consistent across regions while thresholds differ?

What makes this Principal-level: You decouple what changes quickly from what changes slowly and assign ownership of each.

100 million posts a day, 0.1 percent violating, a review budget of 200,000 items a day. How do you decide what humans see?

Rank the review queue by expected harm prevented per review, not by model score. Expected value of reviewing an item is roughly p times predicted reach times severity. Example: item A has p = 0.50 and 100 expected views (50 harm-views); item B has p = 0.05 and 10,000 expected views (500 harm-views). B is the better use of a reviewer even though its score is lower.

Then close the loop: auto-action above a high-confidence threshold, queue by value density between thresholds, and ignore the low-value remainder. Reserve a small random sample for unbiased measurement of precision and recall, since the queue is a biased sample. Recompute capacity-aware thresholds daily as volume and reviewer throughput change.

Likely follow-ups: How do you estimate predicted reach for a post that was just created? · What does the random audit sample cost, and why do you still pay for it?

What makes this Staff-level: You optimize human attention as a scarce resource and keep an unbiased sample for measurement.
