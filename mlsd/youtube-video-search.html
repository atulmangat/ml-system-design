Chapter 4

YouTube Video Search

A user types a few words and expects the right video in the first screen of results, out of roughly ten billion. The query is text but the answer is video, so the system has to bridge two modalities while also exploiting the rich text that already surrounds every video. The core idea is a hybrid: a lexical retriever for exact words and names, a cross-modal embedding retriever for what is actually shown, and a learned ranker that fuses them under a few hundred milliseconds.

Clarifying Requirements

Search is a deceptively open problem: autocomplete, voice, personalization, ads, and safety all hide inside the word "search". Spend the first few minutes pinning down the slice you are asked to build, because every answer below changes an architectural decision.

Candidate

Is the input always a typed text query, or do we also take voice, an image, or a video clip?

Interviewer

Typed text only. No voice, no image queries.

Candidate

Which languages must we support?

Interviewer

English only, both for queries and for the videos you index.

Candidate

How large is the catalog, and what do we know about each video?

Interviewer

Around ten billion videos, from a few seconds to several hours. Each has a title, description and tags, and many have auto-generated captions.

Candidate

May the model look at the video content itself, or only at the text metadata?

Interviewer

Both. Using the visual content is part of the point of the exercise.

Candidate

Is the ranking personalized?

Interviewer

No. Two users typing the same query get the same list. That also makes caching straightforward.

Candidate

What supervision exists? Click logs, watch time, human relevance labels?

Interviewer

You have search logs with impressions, clicks and watch time, and a modest human-rated set meant for evaluation, not training.

Candidate

What are the latency and traffic targets?

Interviewer

A few hundred milliseconds end to end. For traffic, assume about a billion searches a day.

Candidate

How fresh must the index be? If someone uploads a video, when must it be findable?

Interviewer

Metadata search within minutes; content-based search can lag somewhat. Trending news events are the hard case.

Candidate

What is out of scope: autocomplete, ads, safety, searching inside a video?

Interviewer

All out. Assume a separate policy system hands you a "blocked" flag per video. Focus on retrieval and ranking.

Build a video search system. The input is an English text query and the output is a ranked list of videos from a catalog of about ten billion. There is no personalization. Training signals are click and watch logs; a small human-rated set is for evaluation. Titles, descriptions, tags, captions and the video content are all usable, and results must arrive within a few hundred milliseconds at about a billion searches per day.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

"Show relevant videos" can be turned into several different trainable objectives. Each one defines what the model is rewarded for, so the choice deserves a short comparison in an interview.

Candidate objectives for video search
ObjectiveWhat it optimizesProsCons
Predict P(click | query, video)Probability the user clicks a resultHuge volume; trivial to logRewards clickbait titles and thumbnails; heavily position-biased
Predict P(qualified watch | query, video)Probability of a click followed by meaningful viewingMuch cleaner relevance proxy; punishes bounceFavors long and autoplay-friendly videos; delayed signal
Predict human relevance gradeGraded 0 to 3 judgement from ratersClosest to true relevance; also usable for any metricSmall and expensive; cannot cover the tail of queries
Similarity of query and video in a shared spaceCosine of a text vector and a video vectorIndexable with ANN; works at 10B scale; no metadata neededFuzzy for exact names; needs video-text pairs to train

Decision: the system's objective is to rank videos by relevance to the query. We operationalize it in two layers. Retrieval learns a shared text-video similarity from engagement pairs (click plus qualified watch). The final ranker learns a graded relevance score from the same logs, and the human-rated set audits both.

Input and output

The input is a short string, typically two to five words. The output is an ordered list of video IDs. Internally we run two retrievers whose candidate lists are merged, because they succeed and fail on different queries.

ML category

Several model families can score a (query, video) pair. They differ mostly in whether the video side can be precomputed, which decides whether they can touch all ten billion items.

Model families for scoring query-video relevance
FamilyHow it scoresPrecompute video side?Best use here
Lexical (BM25)Term matching with frequency and length statistics over metadata and captionsYes (inverted index)Retriever for exact words, names, titles
Dual encoder (two towers)Cosine of independently computed text and video embeddingsYes (vector index)Retriever for meaning and visual content
Cross-encoderOne transformer attends over query and video jointlyNoOptional re-scorer of the top 20 to 50
Learning-to-rank treesBoosted trees over many hand-built featuresPartly (features cached)Final ranker over the merged candidates

We choose a dual encoder for the neural retriever. Both towers map into one embedding space where a query vector sits near the vectors of videos that answer it, so retrieval is a nearest-neighbor lookup over precomputed video vectors.

Say why you want both retrievers before drawing boxes: "BM25 is unbeatable when the user types the exact title or a rare name; embeddings win when the vocabulary differs or the metadata is thin. Their failures are mostly uncorrelated, so a union plus a learned ranker beats either alone." Interviewers listen for the uncorrelated-errors argument.

Text preprocessing. Raw queries are messy: casing, accents, stray punctuation, typos. The same normalization is applied to indexed text so that both sides agree. After that the two branches diverge: the lexical branch wants whole-word tokens that can be stemmed, and the neural branch wants subword pieces that map to ids.

Tokenization options for the neural branch
SchemeVocabularyTypos and new wordsCost
Word-levelHundreds of thousands, still misses namesUnknown word becomes an out-of-vocabulary tokenShort sequences, huge embedding table
Character-levelUnder 200Always representableLong sequences, weak word-level semantics
Subword (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece)Typically 30K to 50KA typo decomposes into known pieces and stays close to the correct formSlightly longer sequences than words

Subword wins: a misspelling such as "sourdogh" splits into a few known pieces and still lands near "sourdough", while a pure word vocabulary would map it to an unknown token. The lexical branch stays word-based because posting lists are keyed by whole terms, and misspellings are fixed earlier by the spelling corrector (see Serving).

Video preprocessing. A 10-minute video at 30 fps has 18,000 frames. Encoding all of them is wasteful because neighboring frames are nearly identical. The standard approach is to decode, sample a small fixed number of frames, resize and normalize them, and stack them into a tensor. In parallel, the audio track goes through ASR to produce a transcript that becomes one more indexed text field.

Sampling choices matter more than they look. Uniform sampling (16 frames spread evenly) is the cheap default, but it can miss short events, and it treats a 30-second clip and a 3-hour stream identically. Better options are scene-change-aware sampling (take one frame per shot) and, for long videos, indexing several segment vectors per video, for example one per few minutes up to a cap, and taking the best segment score at query time. The capacity math below assumes one vector per video; segment vectors multiply that cost.

Do not sample frames at a fixed rate such as one per second without a cap. A 3-hour livestream would yield 10,800 frames to encode, and one pathological upload can dominate your indexing cluster for hours. Always cap frames per video and per segment.

Labels

The supervision for both the retriever and the ranker comes from logs. The table compares the available sources.

Label sources compared
SourceProsConsUse
ClickPlentiful; immediateClickbait inflates it; strongly position-biased; says nothing about satisfactionWeak positive only
Click plus qualified watchCleaner relevance proxy; filters bouncesBiased toward long videos; delayed; noisy for background-listeningMain training positive
Human ratingsHighest quality; gradedSmall, expensive, staleEvaluation and calibration, not bulk training

We define a qualified watch as a click followed by watch time of at least min(30 s, 50% of the video length). The min() keeps short videos from being impossible to qualify and long videos from needing a huge watch. Examples from the log above: the click on v2 with 310 s of a 340 s video is a qualified watch; the click on v1 with 12 s of a 612 s video is a bounce.

Position bias. Users click rank 1 far more than rank 5 even when rank 5 is equally good. A click at a low position is therefore stronger evidence than a click at the top. Inverse propensity weighting corrects for it by weighting each positive by one over the probability that its position was examined. Propensities are estimated from a small randomization experiment (for example swapping adjacent results in a fraction of traffic), as in the unbiased learning-to-rank literature.

If the examination probability at position 1 is 1.0 and at position 5 is 0.35, a positive at position 5 gets weight 1 / 0.35 = 2.86 while one at position 1 keeps weight 1.0. Weights are clipped (for example at 10) to prevent a few rare clicks from dominating the gradient.

Noise and delay. Clickbait survives the qualified-watch filter when the video itself is also engaging, so add a text-video agreement check: drop positives whose title has low similarity to the query under a small baseline model. Watch time arrives late (the video must be watched), so the training window ends a day or two before the data cutoff. Selection bias also bites: we only see logs for videos the current system showed, so a new model never learns about good videos the old one ranked low. Reserve a small exploration slice (for example 0.5% of traffic gets one randomized slot) to gather unbiased data.

Model Development

Text retriever: inverted index and BM25

The lexical retriever is a mature, training-free component. An inverted index maps each term to a posting list of the videos that contain it, with per-document term frequency. At query time we fetch the posting lists of the query terms, combine them, and score with BM25. BM25 rewards a term that is frequent in the document (with saturation), rare in the corpus (high IDF), and discounts long documents, using two knobs usually set to k1 around 1.2 and b around 0.75.

Multi-field text. A video has several text fields with very different reliability. Weight them instead of concatenating: a match in the title is stronger evidence of relevance than a match in a 2,000-word description full of links and boilerplate.

Field weighting for the lexical retriever (starting weights, tuned later by the ranker)
FieldStarting weightWhyRisk
Title3.0Short, curated, most preciseClickbait wording
Tags2.0Uploader intent, conciseKeyword stuffing
Channel name1.5Navigational queries ("mkbhd review")Common words in names
Description1.0Long and informativeBoilerplate, links, promos

The ASR field matters more than its low weight suggests: it is often the only text describing the content of a lecture or tutorial titled "Part 2". Speech models trained on large amounts of weakly supervised audio, such as Whisper, give usable transcripts for English at scale.

Cross-modal retriever: two towers

We follow the CLIP recipe: one encoder per modality, both projecting into the same space, trained so that matching pairs score higher than non-matching ones. The video tower reuses an image encoder per frame and then pools across time.

Options for the video tower
OptionIdeaProsCons
Frame encoder plus mean poolingEncode each frame with an image model; average the vectorsCheap; reuses pretrained image-text models; simple to scaleIgnores order; cannot tell "sit down" from "stand up"
Frame encoder plus temporal transformerA small transformer over the frame vectorsModels order and importance of framesMore parameters and training data needed
Video-native space-time attentionAttention across both space and time inside the encoder (Frozen in Time style)Strongest temporal modelling; trains on images and videos togetherHighest compute per video
Cross-encoder over query and framesJoint attention between words and framesMost accurate scoringNo precompute, so unusable beyond a few dozen candidates

Decision. Frame encoder plus temporal pooling, initialized from a pretrained image-text model and fine-tuned on our pairs. Published comparisons such as CLIP4Clip contrast parameter-free mean pooling with sequential and cross-attention heads and show that a strong image-text backbone with simple pooling is a very competitive baseline, so add temporal modelling only where an ablation on your own long-video queries says it pays. Frozen in Time shows the other path: train a space-time encoder on images (as one-frame videos) and videos jointly. A cross-encoder can optionally re-score the top 20 to 50 if latency allows.

Training data and loss

Each training example is a (query, video) positive from the label rule above. The key trick is to get negatives for free from the batch. In a batch of N positive pairs, the video paired with query j is a negative for query i whenever i differs from j. That yields an N-by-N similarity matrix whose diagonal holds the true pairs.

The loss is a symmetric softmax cross-entropy: for each query, treat the N videos as classes and ask the model to pick the right one, then do the same from each video over the queries, and average. A learned temperature scales the similarities so the softmax is sharp enough to learn from.

One query against three videos, temperature 0.1. Similarities [0.62, 0.30, 0.10] become logits [6.2, 3.0, 1.0]. The exponentials are 492.8, 20.1 and 2.7 (sum 515.6), so the correct video gets probability 492.8 / 515.6 = 0.956 and the loss is -ln 0.956 = 0.045. If the model had ranked the true video second (similarities [0.30, 0.62, 0.10], the true video being the first), the true video's probability drops to 20.1 / 515.6 = 0.039 and the loss jumps to -ln 0.039 = 3.24. Larger batches mean more negatives per example and a harder task, which is why CLIP-style training uses very large batches.

In-batch negatives are mostly easy ("tiling" vs a yoga video). Better models need hard negatives: videos shown for the query that users skipped, and near-misses the lexical retriever ranks high but that were not clicked. Hard negatives bring a risk: a video that users skipped may still be relevant (they never saw it, or a duplicate got the click). Filter candidates using the human-rated set and a high-confidence relevance model before treating them as negatives.

Beware false negatives inside the batch. If "sourdough bread" and "sourdough starter" queries land in the same batch, each one's positive video is a perfectly good answer to the other, yet the loss pushes them apart. Deduplicate near-identical queries per batch, or group similar queries so they never co-occur.

Fusion layer and final ranking

Each retriever returns about 300 candidates. Their raw scores are not comparable: BM25 is unbounded (for example 14.2) while cosine lives in [-1, 1] (for example 0.31). The recall gain from merging them is large because each retriever surfaces videos the other misses.

Ways to fuse the two candidate lists
MethodHowProsCons
Weighted score sumw1 * BM25 + w2 * cosineSimpleScores on different scales; weights drift when models change
Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)Sum of 1 / (k + rank) across listsNo score calibration; robust; no trainingIgnores score margins; treats retrievers equally
CascadeText filter, then semantic rerank (or the reverse)CheapThe first stage caps recall of the second
Learning to rankModel over many features, including both scores and the RRF rankBest quality; can use freshness, quality, match featuresNeeds labels and monitoring

RRF scores each video as the sum, over the lists in which it appears, of 1 / (k + rank), with k = 60 as the commonly used constant from the original paper. A video near the top of both lists beats a video that tops only one, and nobody needs to compare BM25 with cosine.

Text list: A, B, C, D (ranks 1 to 4). Cross-modal list: C, A, E, F (C at 1, A at 2, E at 3, F at 5 after a duplicate at rank 4 was filtered out). With k = 60: A = 1/61 + 1/62 = 0.01639 + 0.01613 = 0.03252. C = 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.01587 + 0.01639 = 0.03227. B = 1/62 = 0.01613. E = 1/63 = 0.01587. D = 1/64 = 0.01563. F = 1/65 = 0.01538. Merged order: A, C, B, E, D, F. Larger k flattens the differences between ranks; smaller k makes the top ranks dominate.

Decision. Use RRF as the baseline merger and as the fallback when the ranker is unavailable, and a learning-to-rank model as the final ranker. LambdaMART (boosted trees trained with a ranking objective) is the practical default: it handles heterogeneous features, trains on lists grouped by query, and its gradients are scaled by how much swapping two results would change nDCG, so mistakes near the top cost more than mistakes lower down.

Training labels for the ranker are graded from the logs: 3 for a qualified watch, 1 for a click without a qualified watch (a weak signal that still beats no click), 0 for a seen but skipped result, with propensity weights for position. The human-rated set is not used to train; it checks that the learned ranker does not drift from true relevance.

Evaluation

Offline metrics

Use different metrics for the two stages. Retrieval needs a recall-style metric over a large cutoff; the final ranking needs order-sensitive metrics at the top of the page. For each metric the table gives the definition, a hand-checkable example, the flaw, and the decision.

nDCG@5 by hand. The five results have grades [3, 2, 3, 0, 1]. Gain of a grade g is 2^g - 1 and the position discount is 1 / log2(i + 1). DCG = 7/1 + 3/1.585 + 7/2 + 0/2.322 + 1/2.585 = 7 + 1.893 + 3.5 + 0 + 0.387 = 12.780. The ideal order of the same five videos is [3, 3, 2, 1, 0]: IDCG = 7 + 7/1.585 + 3/2 + 1/2.322 + 0 = 7 + 4.417 + 1.5 + 0.431 = 13.347. nDCG@5 = 12.780 / 13.347 = 0.957. The loss comes almost entirely from placing the grade-2 video ahead of a grade-3 one.

Slices matter. Report every metric by query class (head, torso, tail; navigational versus how-to), because a gain on head queries can hide a regression on rare ones. The lexical retriever usually dominates the head and the embedding retriever the tail.

Interviewers like the "offline-online mismatch" answer. Offline nDCG is measured against raters who judge topical relevance; users also care about quality, recency and watchability. A model can improve nDCG by two points and leave qualified-watch flat, or lift CTR by promoting clickbait and hurt completions. Say that you gate launches on online metrics and use offline metrics for fast iteration and as a regression test.

A/B test pitfalls. Randomize by user, not by query, so each user has a consistent experience. Key every cache by experiment arm, otherwise the control arm's cached results leak into the treatment arm. Run at least one to two weeks to cover weekly seasonality and the novelty effect. Check the ratio of users in each arm (sample ratio mismatch). Interleaving experiments, which mix results from two rankers into one list and credit the clicks, detect ranking differences with far fewer queries and make a good first online stage before a full A/B.

Query understanding

Before retrieval, the raw string goes through a short pipeline. Spelling correction uses the query log: candidate corrections within a small edit distance are ranked by how often they are typed, so "sourdogh starter" becomes "sourdough starter" when the corrected form is far more frequent. Auto-correct only above a confidence threshold, and otherwise show "Did you mean". Language detection routes non-English queries to a fallback. An intent classifier distinguishes navigational queries (a channel or a specific title) from topical and how-to ones, which changes the ranker's feature weights, and entity tagging boosts channel and name matches.

Indexing pipelines

Both indexes are built offline, as a streaming job per upload. The video path decodes and samples frames, runs the video tower, stores the vector in an embedding store, and appends it to the ANN index. The text path runs ASR on the audio, merges the transcript with the metadata, analyzes each field (normalize, tokenize, stem), and writes to the inverted index.

Index update latency. Metadata reaches the inverted index within about a minute, so a new video is findable by its title almost immediately. Frame encoding waits for transcoding and is assumed to take a few minutes, and ASR plus its text indexing perhaps ten to fifteen minutes, so content-only matches lag. New vectors go into a small delta index searched together with the big one and merged during a nightly compaction.

ANN sharding

A 720 GB quantized index does not fit on one machine, so it is partitioned. The simplest scheme is random (hash) sharding: every shard holds an equal slice, a broker fans out each query to all 100 shards, each returns its local top-k, and the broker merges. Cluster-based sharding routes a query to only the nearest 10 to 20 shards, cutting compute, but creates hot shards and complicates rebalancing, and recall depends on the router. Within each shard, an IVF or graph index (for example HNSW) with product quantization gives milliseconds per query at the cost of a small recall loss.

Compute for the vector side. Peak 35K QPS with a 40% cache hit rate sends 21K QPS to retrieval. Fan-out to all 100 shards makes 2.1 million shard-queries per second. At an assumed 5 ms of CPU each, that is 10,500 core-seconds per second, so about 10,500 cores before replication and headroom. Cluster-based routing to 15 shards would cut this to about 1,600 cores, at the price of router-recall loss and hot spots.

Caching popular queries

Because results are not personalized, they can be cached. A result cache keyed by the normalized query plus region and safe-search flags stores the final ranked ids, with a short TTL for trending queries (minutes) and a longer one for stable ones (an hour or more) plus invalidation on policy takedowns. A second, cheap cache stores query embeddings so the encoder is skipped for repeats. The hit rate follows the heavy tail of search traffic: a small number of head queries account for a large share of volume, but there is always a tail. Search engines have publicly said that a sizeable fraction of daily queries, on the order of 15%, have never been seen before.

Effect of the cache at a 40% hit rate (10M entries). Backend load drops to (1 - 0.40) x 35K = 21K QPS. Mean latency with a 5 ms hit and a 163 ms miss is 0.40 x 5 + 0.60 x 163 = 2 + 97.8 = about 100 ms, down from 163 ms. Raising the hit rate to 50% would reduce the backend to 17.5K QPS and the mean to 84 ms, but only by caching far more of the tail, with diminishing returns.

Freshness and trending

Nightly rebuilds are too slow for breaking news. A fast path indexes new uploads and recent signals into a small real-time index that is searched together with the main index and merged at query time. Queries whose volume spikes are detected from the log stream; for them, cache TTLs shrink and a recency feature is boosted in the ranker. The main index absorbs these videos at the next compaction.

Model refresh, re-embedding and monitoring

The ranker retrains weekly on the latest logs; the two-tower model retrains monthly or when offline gates say so. An encoder upgrade changes the embedding space, so query vectors from the new model cannot search an index built by the old one. The safe pattern is blue-green: build a complete new index in the background, run the new query encoder against it in shadow traffic, then switch both together. Re-embedding cost, as an assumption: 10B videos x 16 frames = 160B frame passes; at 2,000 frames per GPU-second that is 8 x 10^7 GPU-seconds, about 22,200 GPU-hours, or roughly 22 hours on 1,000 GPUs, plus the often larger cost of decoding video. Training the new model to stay aligned with the old space (so old vectors remain usable) avoids a full rebuild but constrains quality.

Monitor: recall of ANN versus exact search on a sampled set (index health), score and embedding distribution drift, zero-result and reformulation rates, cache hit rate, and index freshness (age of the newest searchable upload). Re-run the human-rated set after each model or index change as a regression gate.

Other Talking Points

  • Moment-level retrieval: search for a segment inside a long video using per-segment vectors and ASR timestamps, so a result can start at the right second; it matters because many queries are really "where in this video".
  • Multilingual search: use a multilingual text encoder and transliteration, so queries in another language still map into the same video space.
  • Personalization: add a user tower or user features in the ranker only, and keep retrieval shared; it matters because personalization defeats result caching.
  • Autocomplete and "did you mean": a separate system trained on query logs, but its suggestions shape the distribution of queries the search sees.
  • On-screen text (OCR) and audio events as extra fields; it matters for videos with slides, code or signage that no transcript captures.
  • Debiasing the click logs with counterfactual learning and exploration traffic; it matters because the training data comes from the old ranker's choices.
  • Safety and policy filters applied after ranking and in the cache; a takedown must also purge cached pages quickly.
  • Distillation of the video encoder and the use of int8 or product-quantized embeddings; it matters because index memory and re-embedding cost scale with ten billion items.
  • Query-level evaluation with interleaving, which needs far fewer impressions than a full A/B to detect a ranker improvement.

Questions are tagged by the level they target. The first set checks Senior-level fundamentals and arithmetic; Staff and Principal questions probe trade-offs, operations, experimentation and organization. Try to answer aloud for two minutes before opening one.

Why combine text search and embedding search instead of picking the better one?Their errors are mostly uncorrelated. Text search is precise on exact words and names and resistant to visually ambiguous content, but fails on vocabulary mismatch and on videos with thin metadata. The embedding retriever matches meaning and visual content but is fuzzy on rare entities and on videos that look static. The union reaches higher recall than either list, and the ranker uses features from both to order the merged set.
Compute the RRF score (k = 60) for a video at rank 2 in the text list and rank 5 in the cross-modal list, and compare it to a video at rank 1 in the text list only.The first video scores 1/62 + 1/65 = 0.01613 + 0.01538 = 0.03151. The second scores 1/61 = 0.01639. The video found by both retrievers wins even though it ranks lower in each list, which is the agreement effect RRF is designed to produce.
A ranking has grades [2, 3, 0] for its top three results. What is nDCG@3?DCG = (2^2 - 1)/1 + (2^3 - 1)/log2(3) + 0 = 3 + 7/1.585 = 3 + 4.417 = 7.417. The ideal order is [3, 2, 0]: IDCG = 7 + 3/1.585 = 7 + 1.893 = 8.893. nDCG@3 = 7.417 / 8.893 = 0.834. Swapping the top two results costs about 17% of the metric.
At 40K peak QPS, a cache with a 50% hit rate has a 4 ms hit and 160 ms miss. What are the backend load and the mean latency?Backend load is 0.5 x 40K = 20K QPS. Mean latency is 0.5 x 4 + 0.5 x 160 = 2 + 80 = 82 ms. Also state that p99 is dominated by misses, so the cache helps the mean and load far more than tail latency; hedged requests and degradation handle the tail.
The new model improves offline nDCG@10 by 2 points but qualified-watch rate is flat in the A/B test. What could explain this and what would you do?Raters judge topical relevance; users also respond to freshness, video quality, length and watchability that the offline set does not measure. The offline set may also miss the slices where the model changed (for example tail queries), or position bias in logs inflated the offline signal. Check metrics by query slice, compare the human grades of the results that changed, inspect clickbait and duration shifts, and add features or labels that capture the missing aspect before shipping.
You ship a better text-video encoder. How do you roll it out without downtime, and what does it cost?Query vectors from the new model live in a different space than the old index, so you rebuild the entire ANN index (blue-green): re-encode every video with the new video tower, build the new index in the background, test with shadow traffic and the human-rated regression set, then switch the query encoder and the index together. Under the stated assumptions the encode pass is about 160B frame passes, around 22,000 GPU-hours (decoding extra). A compatible-training approach that keeps the new space aligned to the old one avoids the rebuild at some cost in quality.
The new model improves tail queries but regresses the head. Product wants one launch decision. How do you decide?

Break results by query frequency and intent. Head queries are often navigational (a channel name, a famous video) and are served by caches and exact match, so a small regression there hits many users. Tail queries are where embeddings help most and where the aggregate metric hides little. Report an impact-weighted metric (by traffic) and a tail-equity metric (average over queries, so tail counts equally) and decide with a rule that was written before the test.

Do not accept a single blended number if slices move in opposite directions (the Simpson's paradox trap). Fix the regression: route by query class (keep lexical retrieval dominant for navigational queries), use class-aware fusion weights, or ship the model only for the tail class first. Validate with a stratified A/B test, then expand.

Likely follow-ups: How do you classify a query as head or navigational? · What if the head regression is only in one language?

What makes this Staff-level: You make the decision rule explicit and use slices and routing to avoid an all-or-nothing launch.

A user types "jaguar". How does the system handle ambiguity, and how do you evaluate it?

Estimate an intent distribution for the query from logs (animal, car, band, a game) and retrieve for several intents instead of one. At re-rank, diversify when intent entropy is high (maximal marginal relevance or intent-aware methods), with personalization as a tie-breaker, not the main driver, so the same query behaves predictably. For low-entropy queries, skip diversification.

Evaluate with intent-aware metrics (alpha-nDCG or expected reciprocal rank with intents) on a judged set that includes ambiguous queries, plus online signals: reformulation rate, abandonment, and time to first qualified watch. Watch that diversification does not hurt clear queries.

Likely follow-ups: How would you build the intent distribution for a brand-new query? · What is your guardrail against over-diversifying?

What makes this Staff-level: You treat ambiguity as a modeled quantity with its own metric, not a corner case.

How would you use LLMs in this system without breaking latency or cost budgets?

Offline first. Use an LLM to produce graded relevance labels for query-video pairs, calibrated against a human gold set (report agreement and known biases such as length preference), to generate synthetic queries for tail training, and to produce summaries and topics from transcripts for indexing. The online model stays small and fast; the LLM's knowledge is distilled into it.

Online only where the budget allows: rewrite rare queries with a cached LLM call (cache by normalized query), and re-rank the top 20 with a distilled cross-encoder if the latency math works. Keep a non-LLM fallback and a hard timeout. Evaluate with a human-checked sample, since an LLM judge can drift or agree with itself.

Likely follow-ups: What is the cache hit rate you need for the rewrite step to pay for itself? · How do you detect that the LLM judge has drifted?

What makes this Staff-level: You place the expensive model where it is amortized and measured, and you keep a safe fallback.

Search and recommendation teams both build video embeddings. A reorg asks you to unify them. Argue for or against.

For: one representation means lower training and serving cost, shared infrastructure (feature store, ANN service), consistent semantics, and transfer from the larger recommendation data to search's tail. Against: the objectives differ (query-conditioned relevance versus personalized satisfaction), release cadences differ, and a shared model couples launches and slows both teams.

A defensible position: share the video tower and the infrastructure, keep separate query or user towers, ranking heads and guardrails. Decide with evidence: an ablation of joint versus separate training measured on both products, the coordination cost in blocked launches, and a compatibility policy for embedding versions. Review it every six months and name the signal that would make you split.

Likely follow-ups: What would the interface between the two teams look like? · How do you handle a launch that helps search and hurts recommendation?

What makes this Principal-level: You state a recommendation, support it with an experiment, and define what would change your mind.
