Chapter 3

Street View Blurring System

A street-level mapping product photographs whoever happens to be on the sidewalk and every car at the curb. Before any frame goes public, the platform must find each identifiable face and license plate and make it unrecognizable. The ML core is object detection, but the real design problem is the operating point: the system is tuned for recall, because one missed face is a privacy incident while one unnecessary blur is a cosmetic flaw, and it must hold that recall across billions of images and be re-run whenever the model improves.

Clarifying Requirements

Start by pinning down what "blurred" means, what a miss costs, and how big the archive is. The answers below are the ones this chapter designs against.

Candidate

Which objects must be hidden?

Interviewer

Human faces and vehicle license plates. Other personal data, such as house numbers or text on screens, is out of scope for the first version.

Candidate

What do the input images look like?

Interviewer

Frames from multi-camera rigs on vehicles. Assume a frame is about 9 megapixels, and several frames are captured at every position along the route.

Candidate

What labeled data do we have?

Interviewer

About one million images with human-drawn boxes around faces and plates. Treat the labels as mostly clean but not perfect.

Candidate

Is this real time or batch?

Interviewer

Batch. Frames are processed after upload and before publication. There is no per-image latency target, but the backlog must clear in days, and the whole archive must be re-runnable when a better model ships.

Candidate

How large is the archive?

Interviewer

Billions of images. Plan with one billion frames for a full pass, and assume 0.4 faces and 0.3 plates per frame on average.

Candidate

What matters more, catching everything or avoiding over-blurring?

Interviewer

Catching everything. A visible face is a privacy incident. But blurring half the street is not acceptable either, so propose a recall target and show how you would measure it.

Candidate

How do we learn about misses after launch?

Interviewer

Users can report an unblurred face, car or home. Reports are reviewed, and an approved blur is permanent. Design the data path, not the report button.

Candidate

What happens when we launch in a new country with different plate formats?

Interviewer

That happens regularly. The system should degrade gracefully and the new region should not publish before it passes an audit.

Candidate

Any constraints on cost or on handling the raw imagery?

Interviewer

Raw frames contain exposed faces, so access is restricted and retention is limited. GPU capacity is large but not free, so show a cost estimate.

Candidate

What is explicitly out of scope?

Interviewer

Video, identifying who a person is, and the report UI. Fairness across skin tone, age and region is in scope.

Build a batch system that takes street-level frames, detects every identifiable face and license plate with recall near 99%, blurs them irreversibly, and publishes only frames that passed. Train on about 1M box-labeled images, run on billions of frames, support full re-runs when the model improves, and close the loop with audits and user reports.

Say the asymmetry out loud in the first minute: a false negative is a privacy leak, a false positive is a slightly uglier picture. That one sentence justifies the metric (recall at a fixed false-alarm budget), the low score threshold, and the audit machinery that follows.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

The business goal is "no identifiable face or plate in published imagery." Several ML formulations could serve it.

Candidate formulations
FormulationModel predictsProsCons
Image classification"contains a face or plate" per frameCheap, simple labelsSays nothing about where; most city frames contain something, so it filters nothing
Object detectionclass, box and score per objectOur labels are boxes; the renderer needs boxes; mature toolingBoxes include background; tightness varies between annotators
Segmentationa per-pixel maskBlurs exactly the object pixelsPolygon labels cost several times more; slower; little privacy benefit over a box with a margin
Detection plus verifiercandidates from a recall-tuned detector, rescored by a cheap second stageRecovers precision lost by running at a low thresholdOne more component to version and monitor; worth it only if false blurs hurt

Train a detector for two classes (face, plate) and choose its score threshold from the precision-recall curve so that recall meets the target. The renderer blurs every box above that threshold. Everything else in this chapter is about making that one decision safe and affordable.

Do not select models by mAP alone. mAP averages precision over the entire recall range, but production runs at a single extreme point (recall near 99%) where two models with equal mAP can differ a lot in false alarms.

Input and output

The model input is a square tile of RGB pixels cut from a frame at native resolution (we justify tiling below), optionally with context such as the camera index and the tile's position. The output is a list of zero or more detections, each with a class (face or plate), a box (x1, y1, x2, y2) and a confidence score. Thresholding happens after the model, so the same network can serve different operating points per region or per class.

ML category

Object detection splits into a few families. All of them can be trained with the box labels we have.

Detector families

A candidate may choose a two-stage detector for accuracy. Here the archive is billions of frames and the hard cases are tiny faces, so a one-stage detector on a high-resolution feature pyramid (RetinaNet-style, stride-8 features for small faces) gives the best cost per recall point, and face-specific one-stage detectors such as RetinaFace show the approach reaches state-of-the-art face detection. Keep a two-stage model as the offline "teacher" for pre-labeling and audits.

Split by location, never by frame. Frames a few meters apart show the same pedestrians and the same parked cars, so a random split leaks near-duplicates into validation and inflates recall. Hold out whole routes or whole cities, and keep at least one unseen country in the test set.

Feature engineering

A detector learns its features from pixels, so "feature engineering" here means choosing the input representation. The decisive choice is resolution. A face is identifiable down to roughly 12 pixels wide (the Google paper surveyed colleagues to arrive at that figure). If we downscaled a 3,584-pixel-wide frame to a typical 640-pixel network input, a 12-pixel face would shrink to 12 x 640 / 3584 = 2.1 pixels and vanish. So the pipeline cuts each frame into overlapping tiles at native resolution, plus one downscaled global pass for faces too large to fit in a tile seam.

Horizontal flips are safe for faces; for plates they mirror the characters, so apply them with low probability. Do not use augmentations that move a box out of the crop without clipping the label.

The decision: train on human boxes, expand with model-assisted labeling (a strong offline detector proposes, annotators fix), and treat audits and reports as the highest-value labels because each one is a case the production model got wrong. Write the labeling rule down. For example, label every face of a real person that a reasonable viewer could identify, including profiles, faces behind glass and partially occluded faces, but not faces on billboards, which go into an "ignore" region so the loss neither rewards nor punishes them.

Class imbalance. A detector scores a huge number of candidate locations per tile and almost all of them are background.

With a RetinaNet-style head on pyramid strides 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128, a 1,280 x 1,280 tile has 1602 + 802 + 402 + 202 + 102 = 25,600 + 6,400 + 1,600 + 400 + 100 = 34,100 locations. With 9 anchors per location that is 306,900 anchors. A tile with one face has about 10 positive anchors (those that overlap it by IoU 0.5 or more), so negatives outnumber positives by about 306,890 : 10, roughly 30,000 to 1. A naive loss is dominated by background.

Three tools fight this: focal loss (Model Development), hard negative mining, and sampling. Faces are not spread evenly over size either. The chart shows an illustrative pattern: tiny faces are a small share of the dataset but, at the production threshold, most of the misses, so the training pool deliberately over-samples them.

Hard negative mining. Run the current model over frames known to contain no face or plate and collect its highest-scoring detections. These are posters, mannequins, statues, reflections and shop signs. Annotators confirm that they are true negatives, and the confirmed boxes are mixed into later batches.

Noise, ambiguity, delay and bias. "Identifiable" is a continuum, and labelers disagree most on tiny, blurred or partly hidden faces. Mark such objects "uncertain" and exclude them from the negative loss instead of forcing a yes or no. Reports arrive weeks after capture, so build the training set from a snapshot and fold late findings into the next one. Label bias is a fairness risk: if annotators work mostly on imagery from a few cities, rare skin tones, ages, head coverings and plate formats will be under-labeled, so sample the annotation queue by region and report coverage per slice.

Training data construction

Every anchor is labeled by its overlap (IoU) with ground-truth boxes. Intersection over union is the area two boxes share divided by the area they cover together. The standard rule: IoU of at least 0.5 with a ground-truth box makes an anchor a positive for that box, below 0.4 makes it a negative, and in between it is ignored.

The batch is not sampled uniformly from tiles. A sampling plan oversamples the cases that drive misses:

Architecture

The backbone (ResNet-50) produces feature maps at several resolutions, and a feature pyramid network (FPN) merges them so every level carries both fine detail and context. Two small convolutional heads, shared across levels, predict a class score and a box offset for every anchor. The final-layer bias for the class branch is initialized so that every anchor starts with a low foreground probability (about 0.01), which keeps early training stable under the extreme imbalance.

Loss function

Training minimizes a classification loss over all non-ignored anchors plus a box loss over positive anchors only, both normalized by the number of positives.

Classification: focal loss. Plain cross-entropy gives every anchor a loss of -log of the probability assigned to the true class (call that pt). Focal loss multiplies it by (1 - pt)γ, with γ = 2 in the original paper, so well-classified background anchors contribute almost nothing and the gradient is spent on the hard ones.

Take one tile with 100,000 easy background anchors, each with pt = 0.99, and 3 hard positive anchors with pt = 0.3. Cross-entropy: each easy anchor costs -ln 0.99 = 0.01005, so the easy total is 100,000 x 0.01005 = 1,005; the positives cost 3 x 1.204 = 3.61, so background outweighs the faces by about 278 to 1. Focal loss (gamma = 2, ignoring the alpha weight): each easy anchor costs 0.012 x 0.01005 = 1.005 x 10-6, so the easy total is 0.1005; each positive costs 0.72 x 1.204 = 0.590, so the positives total 1.77. Now the faces outweigh the background by about 17 to 1.

Box regression. Offsets are trained with smooth L1 (quadratic for small errors, linear for large) and often an overlap-aware term. The generalized IoU (GIoU) loss is 1 - GIoU, where GIoU = IoU - (area of the smallest enclosing box minus the union) / (area of the enclosing box); it gives a gradient even for non-overlapping boxes.

For the boxes in the IoU figure, A = (0, 0, 100, 100) and B = (20, 10, 120, 110): the smallest enclosing box is (0, 0, 120, 110) with area 13,200. The union is 12,800, so the penalty term is (13,200 - 12,800) / 13,200 = 0.0303. GIoU = 0.5625 - 0.0303 = 0.5322, and the loss is 1 - 0.5322 = 0.4678.

The two terms are added with a weight chosen on validation data, and the sum is divided by the number of positive anchors in the batch so tiles with many faces do not dominate. Practical training details: mixed precision, group normalization (small per-GPU batches make batch norm unreliable), multi-scale tile sampling, and a periodic refresh of the hard-negative pool.

Post-processing: NMS or not

A dense detector fires several overlapping boxes for one object. Non-maximum suppression (NMS) sorts boxes by score, keeps the best, and discards others whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold, then repeats. This matters for evaluation and for clean metadata. For blurring itself it is optional, and that is a design choice worth stating.

NMS exists to make a clean list of objects, which evaluation and counting need. A blur renderer needs a mask, and the union of overlapping boxes is the same mask. Skipping NMS removes a failure mode (a real face in a crowd suppressed by its neighbor) and costs only a few redundant boxes. Keep NMS, or Soft-NMS, for the audit and mAP pipelines.

An optional verifier can sit after the detector. In the 2009 design a second stage rescored each candidate with cheap context (camera index, box height versus vertical position, color statistics) so the first stage could run at a recall-heavy threshold. If you add one, never let it veto a very high-confidence detection, since a verifier trained on yesterday's data can overrule a rare but real face.

AP by hand. There are 4 ground-truth faces. Ranked by score, five detections are TP, TP, FP, TP, FP. Precision and recall after each: rank 1: 1/1, 0.25; rank 2: 2/2, 0.50; rank 3: 2/3, 0.50; rank 4: 3/4, 0.75; rank 5: 3/5, 0.75. The fourth face is never found. Take the maximum precision at or beyond each recall level: 1.0 for recall up to 0.50, 0.75 for recall up to 0.75, and 0 beyond. AP = 0.25 x 1.0 + 0.25 x 1.0 + 0.25 x 0.75 + 0.25 x 0 = 0.6875.

Why coverage beats IoU for privacy. A ground-truth face box is 40 x 50 = 2,000 px2. The model predicts a centered 30 x 40 box (1,200 px2). IoU = 1,200 / 2,000 = 0.60, a true positive, but only 60% of the face is covered. With the 20% rendering margin (next sections) the blurred region becomes 42 x 56, which covers the whole face. The 2009 paper made the same point: a hand count of "sufficiently blurred" objects was higher than the automatic box-overlap recall, especially for plates.

Pick the threshold. Target: recall of at least 99% with 95% confidence. At 20,000 faces the standard error of a recall near 0.99 is sqrt(0.99 x 0.01 / 20,000) = 0.0007, so a one-sided 95% bound is 1.645 x 0.0007 = 0.0012 below the estimate. A measured 0.990 is therefore not enough; we need a measured recall of about 0.9912, so we lower the threshold from 0.08 toward 0.06 (or grow the test set). At t = 0.08 the precision of 0.52 means 19,800 true boxes and 18,277 false ones: 0.37 false boxes per frame, so roughly one frame in three carries a stray blur. Cover that stray area: at about 48 x 48 px per box on a 9 MP frame it is 0.37 x 2,304 / 8.7M = 0.01% of the pixels, which is why the visual damage is small.

What recall means at scale. With 1B frames and 0.4 faces per frame there are 400M faces. A recall of 0.89 (the 2009 paper's hand-counted figure on its city test set) would leave 44M faces visible. At 0.99, 4M; at 0.999, 400k. For plates at 0.3 per frame, 300M plates and 99% recall leave 3M. No automatic system reaches zero, which is why audits and user reports are a designed-in second line of defense, not an afterthought.

Audit sample size. To estimate a recall near 0.99 to within +/- 0.001 (95% interval), we need 1.96 x sqrt(0.99 x 0.01 / n) = 0.001, so n = 0.0099 / (0.001 / 1.96)2, about 38,000 faces. At 0.4 faces per frame that is roughly 95,000 audited frames per release, and more for any slice that must be measured as tightly.

You cannot A/B test privacy on users. Showing one cohort unblurred faces to measure the difference is unacceptable, and real incidents are too rare to detect quickly: to see a drop from 2 to 1.5 reports per million views with 80% power at the 5% level takes about (1.96 + 0.84)2 x (2 + 1.5) / 0.52 = 110 million views per arm. Use shadow mode on audited samples to decide, then a staged regional rollout with a holdback region, and treat online report rates as a monitor, not a gate.

Serving

Batch architecture

The system is a throughput pipeline, not a request-response service. A scheduler splits the archive into shards by region and priority; CPU workers decode and tile frames; GPU workers run the detector with large batches; detections are written to a store; and a renderer applies the blur from the stored boxes to the raw pixels, writes a published copy, and a gate decides whether it may go live.

Applying the blur

The renderer takes the union of all boxes above the threshold, expands each box by a margin, and applies an irreversible redaction to the raw pixels. The margin absorbs loose boxes, hair, ears, and plate edges. The redaction must be irreversible and visibly an obscuring effect: the 2009 paper used noise plus an aggressive Gaussian blur, alpha-blended into the surroundings starting at the box edge, and noted that low-strength blur or pixelation can be partly undone. Use a filter strength proportional to box size so a small box is not left readable.

Margin is a recall lever with a visible cost: 20% per side nearly doubles each blurred area (56 x 70 = 3,920 versus 2,000 pixels). Tune it per class on the audit set. Faces usually need more margin than plates, and the margin should be capped so a large false box does not wipe out a storefront. Face obfuscation studies on large image datasets suggest that blurring faces costs recognition models little accuracy, which supports being generous with the blur.

Model refresh and reprocessing

Retrain on a regular cadence (for example quarterly) or sooner when audits show drift in a region or class. A new model only helps published imagery if the old imagery is reprocessed, and a full pass costs about $62k in GPU time under our assumptions, so do it in priority order rather than uniformly.

Audits and the feedback loop

Two loops keep the system honest. The proactive loop samples published imagery on a stratified design (region, lighting, object size, model version), has trained auditors mark every visible face and plate, and reports recall and pixel FPR with confidence intervals per slice. The reactive loop takes user reports, verifies them, applies a permanent manual blur quickly, and feeds the confirmed case into the hard-case store that seeds the next training set and the permanent regression set.

On-request blurring. Beyond automatic detection, people can ask to blur their home, car, or whole body. Treat this as a separate path: verify eligibility (for property, proof of address that is deleted after a short retention), store the approved blur as a geometry override keyed to the panorama, apply it in the renderer after detection and always on reprocessing, and exclude blurred imagery from downstream uses such as generative training. Such a blur is permanent.

Monitoring

  • Detection density per frame by region and camera. A sudden fall in faces per frame means a broken camera, a bad threshold or a model regression, long before a human notices.
  • Score distribution drift. Compare histograms of detection scores per region against a baseline; shifts precede recall loss.
  • Blur rate and pixel FPR proxies. Share of frames with at least one blur, and total blurred area per frame.
  • Audit recall per slice against the release target, with its interval, every release and on a rolling basis.
  • Pipeline health: backlog age, GPU utilization, version coverage (what share of published frames has current-version detections), post-render check failures.

Privacy of the raw imagery

The pipeline itself handles the most sensitive artifact in the product: unblurred faces. Keep raw frames in a separate project with encryption at rest, short-lived service credentials, no human read access except logged, purpose-limited audit and labeling tools, and a retention rule. Keeping raw frames lets you re-blur with a better model, but it also keeps the exposure; a defensible compromise is a long, fixed retention window with an automated delete, plus the ability to rebuild from raw only inside the secure environment. Annotators and auditors should see tiles or crops, not whole frames, in a locked-down tool with no downloads, and their access is audited. Detection metadata is sensitive too, because it records where people were.

Other Talking Points

  • Propagate detections across neighboring frames. Adjacent capture positions show the same person; a face detected in one frame can raise the prior (or lower the threshold) in the next, recovering misses without more compute.
  • Use active learning for the labeling budget. Send frames whose top score sits in the uncertain band (say 0.02 to 0.3) to annotators, because they are the cheapest way to teach the model where its boundary is.
  • Blur before stitching, or handle seams. Redacting individual camera images avoids blurring across panorama seams, where a single face may be stretched over two cameras.
  • Distill the offline teacher. A big two-stage ensemble can pre-label and audit, and a student can be trained on its soft outputs, which narrows the cost-accuracy gap.
  • Treat plates by region, not as one class. Per-country aspect ratio and character set drive detection quality; a plate head with a region embedding is often cheaper than separate models.
  • Handle regional law. Different jurisdictions require different strengths or object categories (house numbers, whole bodies), so the renderer and the threshold should be configurable per region.
  • Know the segmentation upgrade path. If boxes look crude on non-rectangular objects, instance masks or learned inpainting can reduce collateral blur, at higher label and compute cost.
  • Re-check the output. Running the detector again on the blurred image and alarming on any remaining high-score box is a cheap independent check on both renderer bugs and weak blur strength.
  • Do not detect-and-identify. The system should never output embeddings or identities; keeping the interface to boxes and classes limits what a leak could expose.
  • Plan for video and user-contributed photos. Different resolutions and motion blur may need their own thresholds and audits before reuse of the same model.

Check yourself

Questions are tagged by the level they target. The first set checks Senior-level fundamentals and arithmetic; Staff and Principal questions probe trade-offs, operations, experimentation and organization. Try to answer aloud for two minutes before opening one.

1. Why can the blurring system skip non-maximum suppression, and where would you still keep it?A blur needs a mask, and the union of overlapping boxes equals the mask that NMS would produce plus a few redundant pixels. NMS can only hurt recall, because a true face next to another face (IoU above the NMS threshold) gets suppressed. Keep NMS for mAP evaluation, for counting objects, and for compact stored metadata; Soft-NMS is an option when ranking quality matters.
2. Going from 99.0% to 99.5% recall: what do you gain, what does it cost, and would you do it?Faces: 400M x (0.995 - 0.990) = 2M fewer leaks. From the threshold table, recall 0.995 needs t = 0.04, where false boxes per frame rise from 0.366 to 0.808, about 0.44 more per frame, or 442M extra false blurs over 1B frames. That is 221 false blurs per prevented leak. Under our assumed 100x cost ratio the move is not worth it; at a 1,000x ratio it is. The better answer is to shift the PR curve with data, a stronger model or a verifier, instead of paying along the existing curve.
3. The target is 99.5% recall with 95% confidence and the test set has 20,000 faces. Is a measured 0.995 enough?No. The standard error is sqrt(0.995 x 0.005 / 20,000) = 0.0005, and the one-sided bound is 1.645 x 0.0005 = 0.0008, so the measured recall must be about 0.9958. Either lower the threshold (more false blurs), grow the test set (the bound shrinks with the square root of n), or improve the model. Small slices need even more care: a 2,000-face night slice has a standard error of about 0.0022 at 0.99, so the interval is wide.
4. A launch in a new country has an unfamiliar plate format and a few hundred labeled plates. What do you do?Do not publish until an audit passes. Use the existing detector at a lower plate threshold, which exploits the general "rectangle with characters mounted on a vehicle" prior, then run model-assisted labeling to produce a few thousand plates, fine-tune with oversampling and region-specific augmentation, and re-audit with a region-specific sample. Also check false alarms (signs, stickers), since the lowered threshold will raise them. Add the region to the monitored slices permanently.
5. Model v2 improves mAP by two points but its night recall is 1.5 points lower at the same false-alarm budget. Ship it?Not as is. The release gate is per-slice recall, not mAP. Options: train with more night data and re-evaluate; use v1 for dark frames (brightness-gated) and v2 elsewhere; or run both and render the union for risky regions, accepting 2x compute there (union is safe because duplicates cost nothing). Ship v2 globally only when the night slice meets its bound.
6. A new model raises recall from 99.0% to 99.3%. Is a full reprocessing run worth $62,000?Faces protected: 400M x 0.003 = 1.2M, so $62,000 / 1.2M is about $0.05 per additional protected face, before counting plates. But do not run it uniformly. If the top 5% of tiles carry 60% of views, reprocessing that tier costs about $3,100 and removes most of the real-world exposure within hours; the long tail can follow on cheap spot capacity over days. Re-render from raw pixels and union with sticky blurs.
The privacy team wants 99.9 percent face recall and you are at 99.5 percent. How do you close the gap, and how do you prove you did?

Error-analyze the misses by slice: tiny faces, profile and occluded faces, night, motion blur, reflections, faces on posters or screens. Each slice has a different fix. Small faces: higher-resolution tiles and multi-scale features. Hard poses: mined hard negatives and targeted labeling from audits. Borderline frames: a second, heavier model run only when the first model's score lands in an uncertain band, which keeps cost close to the baseline. Temporal propagation across neighboring capture positions recovers faces missed in one frame but seen in the next.

Proving it needs a fresh audit set that was never used for tuning, with doubled annotation because annotators also miss faces (that caps measurable recall). Zero misses in n faces only bounds the miss rate at about 3/n with 95 percent confidence, so 3,000 clean faces bound 0.1 percent; if you expect a few misses you need tens of thousands. Report a confidence interval, not a point.

Likely follow-ups: Your labelers miss 0.2 percent of faces. How does that change what you can claim? · What is the cheapest slice to fix and what is the highest-risk slice?

What makes this Staff-level: You move from one number to slice-level causes, and your claim comes with sample size and confidence.

A regulator asks you to demonstrate that blurring is applied to every published image and cannot be reversed. What do you build?

Make the guarantee structural. The serving path can only read from the redacted store; raw imagery sits in a separate, access-controlled store with short retention. The publish pipeline fails closed: if a frame has no recorded detection job for an approved model version, it cannot be published. Each published tile records the model version and thresholds that produced it, in an immutable log.

Add independent verification: a different detector re-scans a random sample of published tiles and a human audit samples the rest, with alarms on any detected face. Irreversibility is about the transform: use a strong blur or solid fill and destroy the original pixels in the published derivative, because mild blur or pixelation can be partially inverted. Support user-initiated takedown requests with a service-level agreement. This is evidence you can show an auditor: lineage, independent checks, fail-closed publishing.

Likely follow-ups: How do you handle a model bug discovered after publishing? · How do you verify a detector with another detector without sharing its blind spots?

What makes this Principal-level: You design invariants and evidence trails, and you can explain how the system fails closed.

Reprocessing the whole corpus costs about $62,000 per model upgrade. Design a policy for when and what to reprocess.

Value each upgrade as expected harm avoided: faces in the corpus times the recall gain times the cost of a miss, compared to compute cost and the risk of regressions. Reprocess by priority, not all at once: dense urban areas and high-traffic locations first, recent captures first, and regions with new regulation first. Use selective reprocessing: re-run only frames where the old model's top score fell in an uncertain band or where a cheap triage model disagrees.

Lower the unit cost: preemptible GPUs, batching, and incremental checkpoints. Set a trigger policy in advance (for example, reprocess when expected recall gain exceeds a stated threshold or at a fixed annual cadence, whichever comes first) so the decision is not renegotiated each time.

Likely follow-ups: What if the new model finds new faces but also introduces new blur on non-faces? · How would you test the triage model without reprocessing everything?

What makes this Staff-level: You turn a recurring cost into a policy with a value model, a priority order and a way to shrink the work.

Face recall differs across lighting, age and region. How do you measure and act on it without inferring sensitive attributes in production?

Evaluate on a curated audit set where slice membership is known from collection or consented annotation, not predicted at serving time. Use non-sensitive proxies that the system can legitimately condition on: face size, pose, light level, image quality, capture region. Set a floor per slice (for example, no slice below 99 percent recall) instead of optimizing the average, and block launches that break a floor.

Fixes follow the data: collect or synthesize more examples for weak slices, reweight the loss, and prefer a single threshold chosen to meet the floor over per-demographic thresholds, which would require inferring attributes. Document slice results in a model card and review them each release.

Likely follow-ups: Two slices conflict: improving one lowers the other. What do you do? · How do you collect slice data ethically?

What makes this Staff-level: You use slice floors and proxies you can defend, and you can explain what you refuse to build.
