Chapter 6

Video Recommendation System

Design the personalized homepage of a large video platform: for every visit, pick about twenty videos out of billions that this particular person will enjoy, in a few hundred milliseconds. No model can score the whole catalog per request, so the standard answer is a funnel: a cheap, embedding-based candidate generation stage narrows the catalog to about a thousand, a heavy multi-task ranker scores them, and a re-ranker applies diversity, safety and freshness rules. The hard parts are choosing an objective that is not clickbait, correcting the biases hidden in logged data, and serving brand-new users and brand-new videos.

Clarifying Requirements

Candidate

Which surface are we designing: the homepage, the "up next" panel after a video, or search?

Interviewer

The personalized homepage only. A user opens the app and sees a feed of videos; the feed may scroll.

Candidate

What is the business goal, and how do we know a recommendation was good?

Interviewer

Engagement: users should find videos they like and keep coming back. Pure clicks are not the goal.

Candidate

What is the scale of the catalog and the user base?

Interviewer

Billions of videos in total and about ten million new uploads a day. Assume a very large user base, a couple of hundred million daily active users.

Candidate

What do we know about the user and the video at request time?

Interviewer

For users: profile fields such as language and country, device, and a long watch and search history. For videos: ID, title, tags, language, duration, uploader, upload time and visual or audio content.

Candidate

Which feedback signals do we log, and are any of them delayed?

Interviewer

Impressions, clicks, watch time, completion, likes, shares, and "not interested" or dislike. Watch time arrives while the video plays, so it is not available instantly.

Candidate

Latency and freshness expectations?

Interviewer

The homepage must render within a few hundred milliseconds end to end. A new video should be eligible for recommendation within minutes, not days.

Candidate

Do we have to handle new users and new videos with no history?

Interviewer

Yes, both. Cold start is a first-class requirement.

Candidate

Any constraints on content policy, diversity or creator fairness?

Interviewer

Unsafe content must not be recommended, the feed should not be twenty near-identical videos, and small creators should have a path to be discovered.

Candidate

What is out of scope?

Interviewer

Ads, notifications, search and the next-video panel. Also skip the video upload pipeline and moderation models; assume a safety score is available per video.

Build a personalized homepage recommender for a video platform with billions of videos, about ten million uploads a day and hundreds of millions of daily users. Objective: long-term engagement without clickbait. Inputs: user profile, history and context plus video content and statistics. Output: roughly twenty ranked videos per page within a few hundred milliseconds, with diversity, safety filtering, and support for new users and new videos.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

"Engagement" is a business word, not a training label. A candidate has to pick something that can be logged per impression, predicted by a model, and that does not distort the feed. Compare the usual options on one imaginary catalog of four video types.

Objective options for the homepage ranker
ObjectiveProsCons
Maximize clicksDensest signal, every impression has a label, easy to trainRewards misleading thumbnails; users click and leave. Position bias is strongest here
Maximize completed viewsRewards satisfying videosFavors very short videos; long, valuable videos rarely "complete"
Maximize watch timeClosest to time spent, hard to fakeFavors long videos, can reward compulsive content, arrives late and is noisy
Maximize explicit feedback (likes, shares)Clear statement of preferenceVery sparse, under 1 percent of impressions, and skewed to a few active users
Survey or "worth my time" ratingDirectly measures satisfactionTiny volume; usable as a calibration signal, not a primary label
Blend of several predicted behaviorsBalances short-term engagement with satisfaction; tunableNeeds a multi-task model and weights chosen by experiments

Do not train on clicks alone. A click only says the thumbnail and title were attractive; it says nothing about whether the video delivered. A click-trained model learns to find the most tempting packaging, and the platform pays for it in abandoned sessions and lost trust.

Decision. Use a meaningful watch (watched at least 30 seconds or half the video, whichever is shorter) as the main positive for candidate generation, and predict several behaviors in the ranker (meaningful watch, expected minutes, like, share, dislike). The final score is a weighted blend of those predictions, with the weights tuned in A/B tests against retention, not by hand.

Input and output

Conceptually the system is a function: given a user and a context, return an ordered list of videos. Internally it scores user-video pairs, so the practical contract is score(user, context, video), evaluated cheaply for a thousand candidates and exactly for a hundred.

ML category

Recommendation can be framed several ways. The table compares the families a candidate should know; the figures after it show the intuition behind the first three.

Collaborative filtering starts from a user-video matrix. Most entries are unknown, because each user has seen a tiny fraction of the catalog. The task is to predict the blanks.

Matrix factorization (Koren et al., 2009) explains the matrix with k numbers per user and per video. The predicted engagement of user i on video j is the dot product of their vectors, trained to match the observed cells. A two-tower model is the same picture with the embedding lookup replaced by a neural network over IDs and features, which is what lets it embed a video it has never seen.

Our formulation is the multi-stage funnel. The eligible pool is reduced by cheap models that score a lot of items, then by expensive models that score few. This is the structure described in the YouTube papers (Covington et al., 2016) and is what the interviewer expects.

State the counts and the cost per stage out loud. "A 10-millisecond ANN lookup returns 1,000 candidates, a 40-millisecond deep ranker reorders them" shows you understand that the funnel exists to fit a latency budget, not as a ritual.

Data Preparation

Data engineering

Three sources feed the system: the video catalog, the user table, and the interaction log. Assume 200 million daily active users, five homepage loads each, twenty impressions per load. That is 20 billion impressions per day; at about 100 bytes each the impression log grows by roughly 2 TB per day, so 30 days of training data is about 60 TB. With an assumed 5 percent click rate and 2 percent meaningful-watch rate, that is about 1 billion clicks and 400 million meaningful watches per day.

Two logging rules matter more than any model choice. First, log impressions, not just clicks: without the videos that were shown and ignored there are no negatives and no way to measure click rate. Second, log the exact features used at serving time next to each impression, so training sees what the model saw (point-in-time correctness) instead of recomputing features later and leaking the future. Also log the slot, device and the model version, since slot is needed to correct position bias and the version is needed to debug experiments.

Feature engineering

Features come from three entities plus the history. The most important decisions are how to encode high-cardinality IDs and how to summarize a long, variable-length history into a fixed vector.

Embedding table sizes drive memory. A full table for one billion video IDs at 64 dimensions and 4 bytes per float is 256 GB, far too big to replicate to every serving machine and mostly wasted on videos with a handful of impressions. Keeping the 10 million most-seen IDs plus 1 million hash buckets for everything else costs 11M x 64 x 4 B = 2.8 GB. Hash collisions blur the tail, which is acceptable because tail videos are better described by their content features.

Mention "example age" and the point-in-time rule together: Covington et al. feed the age of the training example as a feature and set it to zero at serving, so the model learns that the distribution of watches shifts over time instead of averaging over the whole training window.

Decision. Positives are meaningful watches, weighted by watch fraction or capped minutes; negatives are impressions that were not clicked, or clicked and abandoned within seconds; likes, shares and dislikes become auxiliary heads in the ranker. Click alone is demoted to an input signal for fast feedback.

Four data problems deserve an explicit answer.

  • Imbalance. With a 2 percent positive rate, either weight positives or downsample negatives. If negatives are kept at rate r, correct the predicted probability afterwards: p = p' / (p' + (1 - p') / r). For r = 0.1 a raw prediction of 0.30 becomes 0.30 / (0.30 + 7) = 0.041.
  • Noise. Drop sessions with bot-like patterns, impressions below the fold that the user never saw (use viewport logging), and ultra-short accidental clicks; down-weight shared-device accounts.
  • Delayed feedback. A long video's watch time is unknown for minutes. Log progress heartbeats every 10 seconds, train batch jobs only on impressions older than a maturity window (for example 24 hours), and for streaming updates use a label-delay correction in the style of Chapelle (2014) or treat unmatured impressions as unlabeled rather than negative.
  • Bias. Logged data only contains what the previous recommender chose to show (exposure bias), and top slots get more attention (position bias). Handle position with a shallow tower (below), exposure with a small random-exploration slice that also gives unbiased evaluation data, and popularity bias with sampling correction.

Split data by time, never randomly: train on days 1 to 28, validate on day 29, test on day 30. A random split lets the model see tomorrow's trends through today's data. For each training example use only the history that existed before the impression, and when the target is "the next watch", predict that watch rather than a randomly held-out one, which would leak future information into the history features (Covington et al.).

Model Development

Decision. Two-tower retrieval for candidate generation (plus a few simple sources), a multi-task mixture-of-experts network for ranking, and a rule-plus-greedy-optimizer layer for re-ranking. Each stage can be replaced independently, which is why companies deploy this structure.

Candidate generation: the two-tower model

The user tower turns user features, the averaged watch history and context into a 128-d vector; the video tower does the same for a video. Both vectors are L2-normalized and the relevance score is their dot product divided by a temperature. Because the score separates into one term per side, every video vector can be precomputed and indexed; at request time only the user tower runs.

Training examples come from the logs. Each positive is a (user, video) pair with a meaningful watch. Negatives matter more than any architecture detail, because retrieval has to separate the right video from the billions it never saw.

Negative sampling choices for retrieval

Impressed, not watchedVideos that were shown to the user and ignoredRealistic: the user actually saw themOnly covers what the old system chose; ignoring can be due to position, not dislike

In-batchOther positives in the same batch act as negatives for each userFree (no extra tower passes), batch size 4,096 gives 4,095 negatives per examplePopular videos are over-represented; needs logQ correction

The loss is a sampled softmax. For a batch of B pairs, treat each user's own video as the correct class among the B videos in the batch. Cross-entropy pushes the correct video's score above the others. The matrix below is the batch: row i is the softmax for user i, the highlighted diagonal is the positive, and the other cells in the row are in-batch negatives.

The catch is sampling bias. Videos appear in a batch in proportion to how often they are watched, so a popular video is drawn as a negative far more often than a uniform sampler would draw it. The model then learns to push popular videos down for everyone, which hurts the head of the distribution. Yi et al. (2019) fix this by subtracting the log of each video's sampling probability from its logit: s_corrected = s - log p. The probability p is the video's frequency in the training stream, estimated online with a streaming sketch, because the catalog changes every day and no fixed vocabulary exists.

Take one user with three videos in the batch. Sampling probabilities: A (popular) p = 0.5, B p = 0.125, C p = 0.0625. The user actually watched C. Raw scores: A 1.2, B 0.4, C 1.0. Without correction, softmax gives C a probability of e^1.0 / (e^1.2 + e^0.4 + e^1.0) = 2.718 / 7.530 = 0.361, loss = -ln 0.361 = 1.02. With correction add -ln p to each score: A 1.2 + 0.693 = 1.893, B 0.4 + 2.079 = 2.479, C 1.0 + 2.773 = 3.773. Now C gets e^3.773 / (e^1.893 + e^2.479 + e^3.773) = 43.49 / 62.06 = 0.701, loss = 0.356. The popular video A beat C by 0.2 in raw score, yet the corrected loss is mild: A is the negative that is sampled most often, so beating C is expected of it and should not be punished as hard. The rare positive C is not drowned out by popular negatives.

Uncorrected in-batch softmax pushes retrieval toward the long tail and hides popular good videos, and corrected-but-miscalibrated frequency estimates push the other way. Always check the retrieved set's popularity distribution against the training positives.

Retrieval sources and ANN lookup

One model never gives the best candidate set. Production systems merge several sources, each with a quota, then deduplicate and filter.

The two-tower source needs a nearest-neighbor search over about 100 million video vectors per request. Exact search costs 100M x 128 x 2 = 25.6 GFLOP per query, which is impossible at tens of thousands of queries per second, so use approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search that trades a few percent of recall for a 100x speedup.

Shard the index by video ID across machines so that each shard returns its top K and a coordinator merges them. Keep a small separate "fresh" index for videos uploaded in the last hours that supports instant inserts, and merge its hits with the main index; the nightly rebuild folds them in.

Ranking: multi-task and position-aware

The ranker sees about 1,000 candidates and can afford hundreds of features, cross-features between user and video, and the retrieval score itself. Following Zhao et al. (2019), a single network predicts several behaviors through a multi-gate mixture-of-experts (MMoE, Ma et al. 2018): shared expert sub-networks, and for each task a learned softmax gate that chooses how to mix the experts. This lets related tasks (watch, completion) share structure while unrelated ones (like and dislike) take different mixes, which reduces the negative transfer that a plain shared-bottom network suffers.

The watch head follows Covington et al.: a logistic regression where each positive example is weighted by its watch time and each negative has weight 1. The model's odds then approximate expected watch time, and at serving time the output exp(w.x + b) is used as the watch-time prediction.

Why watch-time weighting works. Suppose a video had N = 100 impressions, k = 5 clicks, and 20 minutes of total watch time. Weighted logistic regression learns odds = (sum of positive weights) / (N - k) = 20 / 95 = 0.2105. Expected watch time per impression is E[T] = 20 / 100 = 0.20 minutes and the click rate is P = 0.05, so E[T] / (1 - P) = 0.2105, which is close to E[T] (1 + P) = 0.21. For a small click rate the learned odds therefore equal expected minutes per impression. Caveat: with a larger P the approximation is loose, and the target still rewards long videos, so cap watch time or normalize it by duration.

The final score blends the head outputs. A linear blend is simple and tunable: score = 1.0 x P(watch) + 0.5 x P(like) + 1.0 x P(share) - 3.0 x P(dislike) + 0.02 x E[minutes], with expected minutes capped at 30.

Clickbait versus value. Video A: P(watch) 0.30, P(like) 0.02, P(share) 0.005, P(dislike) 0.06, E[minutes] 1.0 gives 0.30 + 0.01 + 0.005 - 0.18 + 0.02 = 0.155. Video B: P(watch) 0.22, P(like) 0.08, P(share) 0.02, P(dislike) 0.01, E[minutes] 6 gives 0.22 + 0.04 + 0.02 - 0.03 + 0.12 = 0.370. B outranks A even though A has the higher chance of being watched, because A is disliked more often and rarely appreciated. The weights themselves are tuned with online experiments (or Pareto search over retention and satisfaction), not chosen by hand.

Another trap is position bias: a video in slot 1 is clicked more than the same video in slot 8, so logged clicks mix relevance with placement. Zhao et al. add a shallow tower that sees only position and device features. During training its logit is added to the main tower's logit, so the main tower is free to explain only the relevance part. At serving time the shallow tower is dropped. Randomly zeroing the position input during training (position dropout) keeps the main tower from leaning on it.

Re-ranking

The ranker optimizes each video independently, so its top 20 can be near-duplicates or all from one creator. A last stage reshapes the list with business rules, applied in this order: remove policy violations and demote borderline content, drop near-duplicates and cap videos per creator, then choose a diverse subset, then add freshness and exploration slots.

Maximal marginal relevance (MMR; Carbonell and Goldstein, 1998) picks items greedily: at each step choose the candidate that maximizes lambda x score - (1 - lambda) x (maximum similarity to the items already chosen). Determinantal point processes (DPP) generalize this by modeling the volume spanned by the selected embeddings, with fast greedy solvers (Chen et al., 2018).

MMR with lambda = 0.7. Candidates: A has score 0.80, B has 0.78 and similarity 0.9 to A, C has 0.70 and similarity 0.2 to A. A is picked first. For B: 0.7 x 0.78 - 0.3 x 0.9 = 0.546 - 0.270 = 0.276. For C: 0.7 x 0.70 - 0.3 x 0.2 = 0.490 - 0.060 = 0.430. C beats B even though B has the higher raw score, because B is nearly a copy of A. Lambda is a product dial: higher means more relevance, lower means more variety.

Cold start and exploration

Cold start has two different faces. A new user has no history, so personalization must come from context and a few questions. A new video has no impressions, so it has no behavioral signal and the ID embedding is untrained.

Exploration is a deliberate cost: showing an unproven video displaces a proven one. A bandit policy (epsilon-greedy, upper confidence bound as in LinUCB by Li et al. 2010, or Thompson sampling) decides how much to explore per video based on uncertainty: few impressions means a wide confidence interval, and the optimistic estimate earns more traffic until the interval shrinks. Always log the probability with which each exploratory item was shown, so you can later evaluate policies offline with inverse-propensity weighting.

Evaluation

Offline metrics

Candidate generation and ranking need different offline checks. Retrieval is judged on recall: did the candidate set contain what the user later watched? Ranking is judged on order quality and per-head probability quality. One ranked list is used for all examples below: five recommended videos, of which positions 1, 3 and 5 were watched meaningfully, and the user also watched one other relevant video that was not in the list (so R = 4 relevant in total).

Check the nDCG arithmetic. Hits sit at positions 1, 3 and 5, so DCG = 1/log2(2) + 1/log2(4) + 1/log2(6) = 1 + 0.5 + 0.387 = 1.887. The ideal list would place four relevant items in positions 1 to 4: 1 + 1/log2(3) + 1/log2(4) + 1/log2(5) = 1 + 0.631 + 0.5 + 0.431 = 2.562. nDCG@5 = 1.887 / 2.562 = 0.737.

Offline metrics computed on logged data inherit the old system's bias: the user could only watch what was shown. Offline gains of 1 to 2 percent often disappear online, and the reverse happens too. Use offline metrics to filter ideas, then decide with an A/B test.

Run A/B tests with user-level randomization and a sample size sized for the metric's variance; watch time is heavy-tailed, so use trimming or ratio metrics with variance reduction (for example CUPED). Beware several pitfalls:

  • Novelty effect. Any change in the feed lifts curiosity for a few days. Run at least two weeks and check that the effect does not decay week over week.
  • Interference. Users share one catalog and creators respond to exposure, so a treatment that pulls traffic toward some creators changes the world seen by the control group. For supply-side changes randomize at creator level or use geo or time-split designs.
  • Short-term versus long-term. A ranker that raises today's watch time can reduce next month's visits. Keep a small long-term holdout (for example 1 percent of users who stay on an older policy for months), the approach described by Hohnhold et al. (2015) for ads quality.
  • Metric gaming and seasonality. Compare against a concurrent control, never against last week; track guardrails with sequential testing so a safety regression stops the ramp.

Say which metric would make you ship even if watch time fell: for example a clear rise in survey satisfaction and 28-day retention with a small watch-time dip. It shows you treat engagement as a proxy and not as the goal.

Serving

Architecture and scale

The online path handles a request in about 150 milliseconds of server time; the offline path trains models, rebuilds indexes and refreshes features. Both read the same feature definitions to avoid train-serve skew.

Scale assumptions: 200 million daily users and five homepage loads each is 1 billion requests per day, or 1e9 / 86,400 = 11.6k queries per second on average and about 35k at a 3x peak. Each request scores 1,000 candidates, so the ranker must score about 35 million candidates per second at peak. At an assumed 6 MFLOP per candidate that is about 210 TFLOP per second, which means a few thousand CPU cores or a few dozen accelerators with batching. Batch all 1,000 candidates of a request into one inference call to keep the hardware efficient.

Caching reduces both cost and tail latency: cache the user embedding for a few minutes (it changes only when history changes), cache video features and video embeddings in memory on the ranker, precompute and store the top 100 ranked list per session so infinite scroll requests the next page from the cache instead of rerunning the funnel, and serve popular, region-level lists to unauthenticated or cold users.

Model refresh, freshness and failure handling

  • Refresh cadence. Retrain the ranker daily on a rolling 30-day window and fine-tune on streaming data every few hours. Retrain the two-tower daily; after each retrain re-embed the corpus (100M videos through the video tower is a minutes-long GPU job) and rebuild the ANN index. Insert videos uploaded since the last build into the fresh index within minutes.
  • Feature freshness. Stream the last-hour video statistics and the user's latest meaningful watches into the feature store within a minute. History freshness is what makes the feed react to "I just watched a cooking video".
  • Warm start. New model versions start from the previous weights and are rolled out as shadow traffic, then 1 percent, then wider, with automatic rollback on guardrail breach.

Monitor input drift (feature distributions and null rates), embedding health (norm and cluster shifts), prediction calibration per head and per slice (new users, regions, devices), the share of impressions going to videos younger than 24 hours, index staleness, and end-to-end latency at p50 and p99.

Challenges

Other Talking Points

  • Long-term satisfaction versus engagement: a ranker tuned on watch time can raise daily minutes while lowering retention, so include survey and regret signals in the blend and check them in a long holdout.
  • Reinforcement learning (REINFORCE with an off-policy correction, Chen et al. 2019) optimizes sequences of recommendations directly, but needs propensity logging and careful variance control; mention it as a next step, not the baseline.
  • Sequence models over the watch history (transformer or recurrent) capture intent within a session; place them inside the user tower and measure whether the latency cost buys recall.
  • Creator ecosystem health matters because creators leave if exposure is unpredictable; track impression share and time-to-first-1,000-views for new creators, and consider supply-side experiments.
  • Safety demotion is a score multiplier applied before blending, not a post-hoc patch, so borderline videos fall in rank without being deleted, with appeals and audits to avoid silent over-blocking.
  • Counterfactual evaluation (inverse-propensity weighting on exploration traffic) gives cheaper policy comparisons than full A/B tests, provided propensities are logged.
  • Embedding-table memory and the hashing trick (Weinberger et al., 2009) bound model size, and collisions are a known accuracy trade-off for the long tail.
  • Re-embedding the corpus after every tower retrain is mandatory: user and video vectors from different model versions live in different spaces and cannot be compared.
  • Multi-objective weights should be revisited as the product changes; treat them as a policy with an owner, a review cadence and an experiment history.
  • Kids and sensitive audiences need separate, stricter policies and often a separate candidate pool; a single global model should not decide them.

Check yourself

Questions are tagged by the level they target. The first set checks Senior-level fundamentals and arithmetic; Staff and Principal questions probe trade-offs, operations, experimentation and organization. Try to answer aloud for two minutes before opening one.

Why is predicting clicks a bad sole objective for a video homepage, and what would you use instead?A click measures the packaging, not the content. A click-trained model learns to surface tempting thumbnails and titles, which raises click rate and lowers satisfaction, completion and returning visits. Use a meaningful watch (for example at least 30 seconds or half the video) as the label, weight by watch fraction or capped minutes, add like, share and dislike heads in a multi-task model, and blend them with weights tuned against retention and survey satisfaction in online experiments.
A video is shown 100 times, clicked 5 times, and watched for a total of 20 minutes. What odds does a watch-time-weighted logistic regression learn, and why?Positives are weighted by watch time and negatives by 1, so the learned odds equal the sum of positive weights divided by the number of negatives: 20 / (100 - 5) = 0.2105. This is E[T] / (1 - P) with E[T] = 0.20 minutes per impression and P = 0.05, which is close to E[T] (1 + P) = 0.21. For a small click rate the odds therefore estimate expected minutes per impression, so exp of the model's output can be used as a watch-time score. The approximation worsens for large click rates and still favors long videos unless watch time is capped.
Why do in-batch negatives need a logQ correction, and what happens without it?In-batch negatives are other users' positives, so a video appears as a negative in proportion to its popularity. Popular videos are therefore penalized more than uniform sampling would, and the model learns to score them too low for everyone; the retrieved set drifts toward the tail. Subtracting log p (p is the video's sampling probability, estimated from a streaming frequency sketch) from each logit removes this built-in prior. In the chapter's example the loss for a rare positive falls from 1.02 to 0.36 because the popular negative that beat it is expected to appear and is down-weighted.
The latency budget is 150 ms and ranking takes 40 ms for 1,000 candidates. A product manager wants 5,000 candidates. What do you do?Do not simply multiply the cost: five times the candidates is roughly 200 ms of ranking, far over budget, plus more feature hydration. Options: add a cheap first-stage ranker (a small tree model or distilled network) that cuts 5,000 candidates to 1,000 for the heavy model; first measure whether recall@5000 is meaningfully higher than recall@1000 on held-out data, since the marginal candidates are low-quality; batch and quantize the ranker; or split the work across more machines in parallel. Decide with the marginal recall and with an A/B test of the added cost, not on intuition.
How would you give a brand-new video a fair chance without hurting user experience?At upload compute its embedding with the video tower from title, thumbnail, audio and channel, and insert it into a fresh index within minutes so it can appear in candidates immediately. Reserve a small, capped share of slots (for example 2 of 20 for fresh videos) and choose which new videos get them with a bandit policy such as Thompson sampling, so uncertainty earns exposure. Measure early meaningful-watch and dislike rates; videos with strong engagement graduate to the main index and normal ranking, weak ones fade. Log the exposure probability for later counterfactual analysis and apply safety filters before exploration.
An A/B test of a new ranker shows +3 percent watch time in week 1 and +0.5 percent in week 3. How do you decide?The decay points to a novelty effect, so the week-1 number overstates the long-run gain. Look at the per-week trend, at satisfaction and regret metrics (survey, dislike, return rate), and at guardrails such as diversity and creator exposure. Extend the test or hold out a small share of users for a longer window to measure the settled effect. Ship only if the settled watch-time gain is significant and satisfaction and retention are flat or better, since a gain driven by novelty or compulsive content is not a durable improvement.
Watch time is up in the A/B test, but retention and survey satisfaction are down. What do you change?

The proxy has drifted from the goal. Add a satisfaction signal: collect a small, random sample of survey responses (for example "was this worth your time?"), train a model to predict it for all impressions, and use that prediction as an additional head in the blend or as a constraint. Penalize patterns that inflate minutes without satisfaction (clickbait: high click, short watch after click; autoplay chains).

Measure long term with a persistent holdout of a small percentage of users for months and cohort retention at 28 days. When you need an earlier read, use a surrogate index that predicts long-term outcomes from several short-term metrics, validated against past experiments. Make the guardrail explicit: a launch cannot ship if survey-based satisfaction or retention drops beyond a stated tolerance, even if watch time rises.

Likely follow-ups: How big must the holdout be to see a 0.3 percent retention change? · How do you stop the survey from becoming a new target to game?

What makes this Staff-level: You treat the objective itself as a hypothesis to be tested, with a long-term measurement plan.

How do you set an exploration budget and avoid a feedback loop where the model only learns about what it already shows?

Put exploration where uncertainty is highest and cost lowest: new videos and new users. Use a bandit-style bonus (Thompson sampling or an upper-confidence bonus) at the candidate or ranking stage for items with few impressions, capped per user and per slate position. Reserve a small slice of traffic (around one to two percent) for randomized or lightly randomized slates that log propensities; this gives unbiased data for training corrections and for counterfactual evaluation.

Evaluate with off-policy estimators (clipped inverse propensity scoring or doubly robust) and confirm online. Track the cost of exploration as lost watch time per user and the benefit as faster item discovery; set the budget where marginal learning value equals marginal cost, and revisit as the catalog grows.

Likely follow-ups: Why clip the importance weights, and what bias do you accept? · How does exploration interact with the re-ranker's diversity rules?

What makes this Staff-level: You can quantify exploration's cost, and you build the unbiased data source that makes later evaluation possible.

Several surfaces (homepage, Shorts, notifications) compete for the same user attention. How do you set objectives and avoid local optimization?

Define a user-level objective shared by all surfaces (for example, a satisfaction-weighted engagement per week, with retention as a guardrail) and measure each surface by its contribution to it, not by its own clicks. Use cross-surface holdouts and incremental measurement, because a gain on one surface that cannibalizes another is not a gain.

Organizationally: a metric council owns definitions, changes are versioned, and conflicts are resolved with experiments rather than by team size. Where surfaces genuinely need different behavior (a short-form feed versus a lean-back TV app), share the core models and let surface-specific layers handle the differences.

Likely follow-ups: How do you attribute a session's value across surfaces? · What happens when two teams' launches interact?

What makes this Principal-level: You move the conversation from surface metrics to a user-level objective and say how it is governed.

How do you design recommendations that are safe for teenagers and avoid rabbit holes?

Use age-aware policies as hard constraints and score modifiers: restrict eligible categories, apply stricter safety demotion, and cap repetition of sensitive topics in a session. Add a diversity constraint at re-rank time that limits consecutive items from the same sensitive cluster, and add friction (a break reminder, a topic reset) when a session loops.

Measure it: the share of sensitive-category impressions among at-risk cohorts, repeat exposure rates, and user-reported well-being from surveys, as launch guardrails next to engagement. Be explicit that this trades some engagement for safety, get sign-off from policy and product, and give users controls (reset recommendations, not interested).

Likely follow-ups: How do you know a user is a teen when age is unreliable? · What engagement loss would you accept and who decides?

What makes this Staff-level: You tie safety to concrete mechanisms and metrics, and you name the trade-off and who owns it.
