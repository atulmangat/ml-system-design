Chapter 9

Similar Listings on Vacation Rental Platforms

On a vacation rental platform, a guest who lands on a listing that is too expensive, too small, or booked for their dates needs a fast path to a better alternative. The "similar listings" carousel on the listing page is that path. The hard parts are that every home is unique and can be booked by only one party for a given set of dates, new homes appear daily, and "similar" has to mean "a guest who liked this would also consider that". The core idea is to learn listing embeddings from browsing sessions, in the style of word2vec, and then retrieve neighbours inside the same market under live availability and price constraints.

Clarifying Requirements

This chapter follows the public Airbnb work on listing embeddings (Grbovic and Cheng, KDD 2018) and its follow-ups, but the design is meant to transfer to any marketplace of unique, single-capacity items: vacation rentals, hotels, used cars, real estate. Start the interview by pinning down the product, the signals, and the constraints.

Candidate

What is the business goal of the carousel?

Interviewer

Increase bookings by helping guests who are browsing find an alternative when the current listing is not quite right.

Candidate

Where is it shown, and what is the input?

Interviewer

On the listing detail page. The input is the listing being viewed, plus check-in and check-out dates and guest count when the guest has set them.

Candidate

Should results be personalized to the guest?

Interviewer

Start with a seed-listing model that is the same for every guest. You may mention personalization as an extension, but the first version should not need a user profile.

Candidate

What data do we have?

Interviewer

Logged-in browsing sessions with listing views and dwell time, wishlist saves, and bookings. Assume about 60 million logged-in listing views per day.

Candidate

How big is the catalogue, and how fast does it change?

Interviewer

About 5 million active listings. Tens of thousands of new listings are added each day, and availability and prices change constantly.

Candidate

Must the carousel respect hard constraints?

Interviewer

Yes. Everything shown must be available for the guest's dates, accept their party size, and sit in a similar price band. Showing a sold-out home is the worst failure.

Candidate

What is the latency budget?

Interviewer

The carousel loads with the page. Assume 100 ms at p99 for the recommendation call, and about 30 million carousel renders per day, with peaks around three times the average.

Candidate

What about new listings with no history?

Interviewer

They must be eligible within a day, otherwise new hosts get no traffic and the inventory stays stale.

Candidate

How will success be measured, and what is out of scope?

Interviewer

Carousel click-through and bookings that start from the carousel, without hurting overall bookings. Search ranking, ads, and price recommendations are out of scope.

Given the listing a guest is viewing, return about 12 ranked listings that are behaviorally similar, available for the guest's dates, within a sensible price band, and able to host the party. Train from browsing sessions and bookings over a catalogue of about 5 million listings, support new listings within a day, and answer in 100 ms at p99.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

"Similar" is not self-defining. A candidate may be tempted to say "same neighbourhood, same price, same number of bedrooms", but that is a feature filter, not an objective. The objective we care about is that the guest finds a listing they will book. Several formulations are plausible.

Candidate objectives for the carousel
ObjectiveIdeaProsCons
Attribute similarityRank by distance in a hand-built space of location, price, type, beds, amenitiesNo training data, explainable, works for brand-new listingsWeights are guesses; misses style, vibe, and what guests actually compare
Predict the next clickGiven a listing, predict the listing the guest views next in the sessionPlentiful signal, models real consideration setsClicks can be noisy and position-biased; says nothing about whether the guest likes the result
Predict the bookingGiven a listing, predict which listing is eventually booked in the same tripDirectly aligned with revenueSparse: only about a percent of sessions end in a booking

Decision. Learn co-engagement embeddings: listings that are viewed in the same sessions should be close, and the listing that was eventually booked is treated as a context for every click in its session. Clicks supply volume, bookings supply the business signal. The system retrieves nearest neighbours and then applies hard constraints and a small re-ranker.

Input and output

Treat the system as a function from a seed listing and stay context to an ordered list. The seed listing carries most of the information; dates and guests are used for filtering rather than for similarity.

In an interview, start with the behavioral skip-gram model, because it is the best match to the data you have and it is a small, strong baseline. Then name the two-tower and graph models as the upgrade path for cold start and richer features, and say what evidence would push you there (for example, a large share of traffic going to listings with under a week of history).

Why does behavior beat attributes? Figure 4 shows the intuition. Two listings can have identical specs and price but sit in different cities, so they will never be compared. Two listings can look different on paper, yet guests repeatedly open both in the same session because they share a neighbourhood, a design style, or a view. Co-engagement carries all of that for free.

The chosen formulation: each listing becomes a point in a 32-dimensional space. Distance reflects how often listings co-occur in sessions. Because most sessions stay inside one market, listings cluster by market first, then by type and price within the market.

The timeline below shows two of the rules on a real-looking trace. A short dwell marks an accidental click, and an inactivity gap of more than 30 minutes ends the session. After cleaning, session 1 is (A, B) and session 2 is (D, E).

Cleaning rules matter more than they look. The Airbnb paper drops clicks with under 30 seconds of dwell and discards sessions with fewer than two clicks. The same logic extends naturally to bots and crawlers (impossible click rates, no mouse events), to very long sessions (a single sitting with hundreds of listings is usually a script or a property manager, so cap length and window), and to listings viewed through a host's own link rather than through browsing. Each of these would otherwise create false co-occurrences that pull unrelated listings together.

Sessions are summarised by a co-click matrix. Figure 8 shows a toy market of five listings with row-normalised co-click shares. Two blocks appear: A, B, D form one consideration set (central neighbourhood) and C, E another (beachside). Skip-gram does not build this matrix explicitly, but it learns a low-rank factorisation of something very close to it, which is why it generalises to pairs never co-clicked directly.

Row normalisation. Raw weekly co-click counts for A are B 40, C 10, D 4, E 1, so the row total is 55 and the shares are 40/55 = 0.73, 10/55 = 0.18, 4/55 = 0.07, 1/55 = 0.02. For E the counts are A 1, B 0, C 30, D 6 (total 37), giving 30/37 = 0.81. The matrix is not symmetric after normalisation because rows have different totals.

The same metadata gives us the cold-start path. A brand-new listing has no clicks, so it is not in the vocabulary. Following the Airbnb paper, we find the three geographically closest listings (within 10 miles) that already have embeddings, share the new listing's room type, and fall in the same price bucket, and we average their vectors. The paper reports that this covers more than 98 percent of new listings.

Labels

Skip-gram is self-supervised, so there are no human labels. What differs is where positive pairs come from.

Sources of positive signal
SourceWhat counts as positiveStrengthProblem
Co-clicks in a sessionListings viewed within a window of each other, with enough dwellAbundant: hundreds of millions of sessionsExploration noise, position bias in search results, accidental clicks
BookingsThe booked listing is a positive for every click in its sessionDirectly aligned with revenue, reflects a decisionRare, delayed, host acceptance also matters
Wishlist saves and inquiriesSaved or messaged listings, treated like soft bookingsStronger than a click, much denser than bookingsSaves can be a way to bookmark, not intent to book; behaviour varies by user

Decision. Use co-clicks as the backbone, add the booked listing as a global context for booked sessions (see Model Development), and optionally treat saves as a weaker version of the same trick. Do not train on bookings alone.

Four data issues to name in the interview. Imbalance: booked sessions are a small share, so the global-context term is applied only to them and is not diluted by an aggressive negative ratio. Noise: dwell and length filters, plus a minimum count so that a listing needs a few dozen appearances before getting its own vector. Delay: bookings arrive hours or days after the last click, so the training window ends a few days early, or sessions are re-tagged in a nightly batch. Bias: clicks come mostly from what search showed, so very popular listings dominate. Subsampling frequent items, as word2vec does, reduces this, and the re-ranker can add a mild exposure penalty.

Decision. Ship skip-gram with negative sampling, with the two marketplace adaptations below (booked listing as global context, in-market negatives), plus the metadata-average cold-start fallback. Keep the two-tower model as the next step if cold-start traffic grows.

The word2vec analogy makes the model easy to explain: a listing is a word, a session is a sentence, and the log of all sessions is the corpus. Words that appear in similar contexts get similar vectors; so do listings that appear among similar neighbouring clicks.

Training pairs come from a sliding window. For every click in a session (the centre), listings within m positions on either side become positive context. The Airbnb paper does not hinge on one window size; treat m between 3 and 5 as a starting range that you tune offline.

Pair counts. A session with 7 clicks and window m = 2 yields 2 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 22 ordered positive pairs (edge clicks have fewer neighbours). If the 7th click is the booking, each of the other 6 clicks also predicts the booked listing: 6 extra positive terms, 28 in total. Long sessions therefore dominate the loss, which is another reason to cap session length.

The architecture is two embedding tables, one for listings as centres (input vectors) and one for listings as contexts (output vectors). The score of a pair is the dot product of the centre's input vector and the context's output vector, passed through a sigmoid. Only the input vectors are kept for serving.

Loss and the two marketplace adaptations

For each positive pair the model maximises the log of the sigmoid of the dot product, and for each sampled negative it maximises the log of one minus that sigmoid. Equivalently it minimises the logistic loss on a set of 1-labelled and 0-labelled pairs. Computing a softmax over 5 million listings would be far too slow, so a handful of negatives stands in for the denominator.

Adaptation 1, in-market negatives. Standard word2vec draws negatives at random from the whole vocabulary. For listings, positives are almost always in the same market (guests rarely compare Lisbon to Bali), and random negatives are almost always in another market. The model then solves the easy question, "same market or not", and never learns to separate a studio from a villa in the same city. The Airbnb paper adds a second set of negatives sampled from the market of the centre listing, which sharpens within-market similarity, the thing a carousel needs.

Adaptation 2, booked listing as global context. In a booked session, the booked listing is the strongest evidence of what the guest wanted. The paper adds a term so that every centre click in a booked session also predicts the booked listing, even when it lies outside the window. Over many sessions this pulls the listings that precede a booking toward the listings that get booked.

One training step, by hand. Suppose the centre vector and context vectors give dot products of +2.0 for a positive pair, +1.0 for an in-market negative, and -2.0 for a random global negative. The logistic losses are -ln(sigmoid(2)) = 0.127, -ln(1 - sigmoid(1)) = -ln(0.269) = 1.313, and -ln(1 - sigmoid(-2)) = -ln(0.881) = 0.127 for the global one. The total is 0.127 + 1.313 + 0.127 = 1.567. The in-market negative contributes almost 10 times the loss of the others, and its gradient on the score has magnitude sigmoid(1) = 0.269 against sigmoid(-2) = 0.119 for the random negative. In-market negatives carry the learning signal; random negatives are cheap regularisation.

Evaluation

Offline metrics

There are no per-recommendation labels, so offline evaluation replays history. The Airbnb paper's test: for sessions that ended in a booking, use the listings clicked earlier in the session as the seed, rank the booked listing against the candidates in its market, and record its rank. The better the embeddings, the closer the booked listing sits to the top, and the effect should grow as the seed gets closer to the booking.

The mean rank of 7.8 looks mediocre while hit rate at 10 is 0.80: the two metrics weigh the tail differently, which is why you should report both.

Offline replay inherits the logging policy: candidates and clicks were shaped by what search and the old carousel showed. A model that beats the old one offline may still lose online. Also note that the booked listing may have been the guest's only available option, not their favourite. Treat offline results as a filter for ideas, and spend A/B tests only on the winners.

Two effects must be handled explicitly in an experiment. First, position bias: cards on the left of a carousel get more clicks regardless of quality. Figure 18 shows an illustrative pattern. When you log clicks as training data or compare rankers, record the displayed position, and when you evaluate a new ranker, randomise a small slice of traffic to measure position effects instead of assuming them away. Airbnb's search team describes position bias handling in their ranking work (references 9 and 10).

Second, interference. In a marketplace with unique, single-capacity inventory, treatment and control guests compete for the same homes. If the treatment arm is better at steering guests to a listing, it consumes capacity that control guests would have booked. Naive user-level A/B tests then overstate the gain; Johari and colleagues analyse this bias formally (reference 13). Mitigations include randomising by market or by geographic cluster, running long enough to see saturation, and comparing total bookings per market rather than only carousel-attributed ones.

Sample size. A common rule of thumb for a two-arm test on a rate p is n per arm of about 16 p(1-p) / delta squared, for 80 percent power at alpha = 0.05. For CTR at 3.5 percent and a 5 percent relative lift (delta = 0.00175): n = 16 x 0.035 x 0.965 / 0.00175 squared = 0.5404 / 0.0000030625, about 176,000 impressions per arm. For bookings per page view at 0.105 percent (1,050 per million) and the same relative lift (delta = 0.00005): n = 16 x 0.00105 x 0.99895 / (0.00005 squared) = 0.01678 / 0.0000000025, about 6.7 million page views per arm. At 30 million renders per day split in two, the volume arrives in under a day, but you still run for at least one to two weeks to cover weekly cycles, booking lag, and market saturation.

If you are asked what you would ship first, say: launch the embedding model against the old attribute-plus-search carousel, primary metric bookings per visitor with carousel CTR as the diagnostic, guardrails on host rejection, new-listing exposure, and latency, and randomise by market if you suspect capacity interference.

Serving

The online path is a short chain: look up candidates for the seed, filter them against live inventory, re-rank a small set, log the impression. The expensive work happens offline.

Retrieval cascade and the funnel

Each stage shrinks the candidate set by an order of magnitude. Retrieval is deliberately scoped to the seed's market, which is what makes exact search affordable.

Latency math (assumptions). A market of 50,000 listings at 32 dimensions needs 50,000 x 32 = 1.6 million multiply-adds per query. The largest market, say 300,000 listings, needs 9.6 million, about 2 ms at an assumed 5 billion multiply-adds per second on one core. Brute-force cosine inside the market is therefore fast enough, and the Airbnb paper does the same: it scores listings from the seed's market on sharded machines. Approximate indexes such as HNSW only become necessary for cross-market or very large shard sizes. The Airbnb search team also reports that HNSW was a poor fit for them because of heavy real-time filtering and about 10,000 listing updates per second, and chose an inverted-file index instead (reference 12).

Nightly top-K precomputation is also cheap. Assume 5 million listings with 2.5 million in 50 large markets of 50,000 each, and the rest in small ones. Exact all-pairs inside the large markets costs 50 x 50,000 squared x 32 = 4 x 10^12 multiply-adds, and the small markets add about 10^11. At an assumed 100 billion multiply-adds per second per node, that is roughly 41 seconds of compute on one node plus top-K selection overhead, so it fits easily into a daily job on a few machines.

Index size. 5,000,000 listings x 32 dimensions x 4 bytes = 640 MB; at 64 dimensions it is 1,280 MB, at 128 it is 2,560 MB. A top-100 neighbour cache stores 100 entries of 4-byte ID plus 2-byte fp16 score, 6 bytes each, per listing: 5,000,000 x 100 x 6 = 3.0 GB. All of this fits in memory on a single machine, so the real sizing question is replication for 1,000 peak requests per second (30 million per day, average 347 per second, peak about three times higher), not capacity.

Filters, re-ranking, and the fallback

The cache gives candidates by similarity only. Everything that changes minute to minute is checked live. Availability for the guest's dates comes from the calendar service; price band and capacity come from the listing store.

How many survive? Suppose 60 percent of cached neighbours are available for the dates and 50 percent of those pass price and capacity. Expected survivors from 100 candidates: 100 x 0.6 x 0.5 = 30, comfortably above the 12 needed. In a peak season where only 20 percent are available, 100 x 0.2 x 0.5 = 10, which falls short, so the fallback must be live. For a seed priced at $95 per night, the price band 0.7x to 1.5x is $66.50 to $142.50 per night. The 0.7 and 1.5 are assumptions; tune them on dead-end rate and booking data.

The final order is a blend. Cosine similarity leads, with small contributions for quality (reviews, acceptance rate), price fit, and distance from the seed. A lightweight learned ranker can replace hand weights once enough carousel impressions are logged.

Blend example. Take weights 0.6 similarity, 0.2 quality, 0.1 price fit, 0.1 proximity (assumed). Candidate X: similarity 0.90, quality 0.70, price fit 0.80, proximity 0.90, so 0.54 + 0.14 + 0.08 + 0.09 = 0.85. Candidate Y: similarity 0.93, quality 0.40, price fit 0.50, proximity 0.60, so 0.558 + 0.08 + 0.05 + 0.06 = 0.748. X wins despite lower raw similarity, because Y is poorly reviewed and far off budget.

Refresh. Retrain every day from scratch on a sliding window of several months with a fixed seed, publish embeddings, build the neighbour cache, and run the new-listing job as one versioned release. Availability and price are never baked in; they are read live. Monitoring. Watch embedding drift (mean cosine between a fixed probe set of listings across versions), neighbour overlap at 10 between consecutive versions (an abrupt drop signals a bad run), share of listings with a trained vector versus provisional, cache age, dead-end rate, and carousel CTR by market. Pre-publish checks mirror the Airbnb paper's sanity tests: listings in the same neighbourhood should be closer than listings in different cities, and listings of the same type and price band should be closer than listings of different ones.

Other Talking Points

  • Personalization with recent searches. Start with a seed-only model; later, add the guest's recent searches to the re-ranker or retrieve with a blend of the seed vector and the average vector of recently viewed listings, because guests narrow their taste over a trip.
  • Seasonality. Retrain on a sliding window that follows the season, and consider weighting recent sessions more heavily, because a beach market's consideration sets shift when school holidays start.
  • Dateless pages. About half of listing views may have no dates; when dates are missing, rank by similarity and price only and show an availability hint, since hiding unavailable homes would otherwise be impossible.
  • Multi-modal cold start. Photo and description embeddings can seed new-listing vectors better than a mean of three neighbours, which matters when style is what drives clicks.
  • Graph and two-tower upgrades. PinSage-style or two-tower models inherit the metadata directly and avoid the separate cold-start job, but cost more to train and refresh; move to them when cold-start traffic grows.
  • Price changes. A hot-season price jump changes who a listing competes with; store price-bucket features per day for the re-ranker and cold-start path instead of using stale averages.
  • Explainability. A short reason such as "similar neighbourhood and price" builds trust and helps support debug complaints, and can be derived from shared attributes of the pair.
  • Two-sided metrics. Track host outcomes next to guest outcomes, because a carousel that sends all traffic to a few hosts raises short-term bookings and lowers supply health.
  • Experiment design under interference. Randomise by market where capacity is shared, and read results as market-level totals, because user-level tests in a scarce-inventory marketplace overstate lift.
  • Re-use the embeddings. The same vectors can power email recommendations, search-ranking features, and host tools such as pricing and calendar suggestions, so their quality compounds.

Questions are tagged by the level they target. The first set checks Senior-level fundamentals and arithmetic; Staff and Principal questions probe trade-offs, operations, experimentation and organization. Try to answer aloud for two minutes before opening one.

1. Why sample negatives from the same market as the centre listing?Because positives are nearly always in the same market, random global negatives are nearly always from other markets. The model can drive its loss down by learning market identity alone and never learns to separate, say, a villa from a studio in the same city. In-market negatives are hard cases that force it to encode type, price, and style within the market, which is exactly what a carousel needs. Random negatives are still useful as cheap regularisation.
2. A session has 9 clicks, the last of which is the booking, and the window is m = 3. How many positive pair terms does it generate?Count ordered neighbours inside the window. For n = 9 and m = 3, the pair count is 2 x [(n - 1) + (n - 2) + (n - 3)] = 2 x (8 + 7 + 6) = 42. The booked listing is the 9th click, so each of the other 8 clicks also predicts it as global context: 8 extra terms, 50 in total. Note that some of those 8 already have the booked listing in their window, so the extra term reweights them rather than adding new information. Long sessions dominate, so cap length.
3. How much memory do vectors for 8 million listings take at 64 dimensions in fp16, and why is memory not the bottleneck?8,000,000 x 64 x 2 bytes = 1,024,000,000 bytes, about 1.0 GB. Even with a top-100 neighbour cache (8,000,000 x 100 x 6 bytes = 4.8 GB), the data fits in memory on one machine. The real limits are request throughput, which needs replication, and the freshness of live availability, which cannot be cached.
4. A host adds a listing at 10 a.m. and wants traffic today. What do you do?The daily training job will not include it, so a new-listing job runs frequently (for example every hour) and builds a provisional vector: the mean of the 3 nearest listings in the same market, room type, and price bucket (within about 10 miles, as in the Airbnb paper). Publish it into the embedding store, make the listing eligible as a candidate, and after enough sessions let the daily run replace it with its own vector. To avoid starving new listings, track new-listing exposure as a guardrail. If the market has fewer than 3 neighbours, widen the radius or fall back to the nearest market.
5. Your A/B test shows +4 percent carousel bookings but flat total bookings. How do you interpret it?The carousel may be cannibalising bookings that would have happened through search, or treatment guests may be taking inventory that control guests would have booked (interference). Check total bookings per visitor in both arms, rerun with market-level randomisation, and compare total booked nights per market. A real improvement shows up in totals; a shifted-attribution improvement does not.
6. When would you replace in-market brute force with an ANN index, and which one?When shard size or query rate makes exact scoring too slow, for example a cross-market "similar to this style anywhere" feature over millions of listings. HNSW gives the best speed and recall trade-off when the corpus is stable. For heavily filtered, rapidly updated inventory, an inverted-file index (IVF) may be better, as Airbnb found for its search retrieval. Start from the latency math: at 2 ms per exact query, brute force within a market is hard to beat.
Bookings are rare and clicks are noisy. How do you choose the weight of the booked-listing context, and verify it helps?

Treat the weight as a hyperparameter with a principled check. The booked listing is the strongest signal of what the guest actually wanted, so it is added as a context for every click in the session (the idea used in listing-embedding work at Airbnb, Grbovic and Cheng, KDD 2018), but too much weight makes the space collapse toward popular, bookable homes.

Evaluate offline with a task that matches the product: given the clicked listings in held-out sessions, rank the eventually booked listing among candidates in the same market and compare hit rate and mean rank across weights. Then confirm online with carousel bookings and the guardrails (click-through, unavailable-click rate). Check slices: new listings and low-supply markets, where bookings are scarcest.

Likely follow-ups: What does the embedding look like if the booked weight is too high? · How would you handle markets with almost no bookings?

What makes this Staff-level: You tie a modeling knob to a held-out task that mirrors the product and to slices where it can fail.

Guests see similar listings that are booked for their dates or three times the price. How do you make availability and price first-class?

Apply hard constraints before ranking, not after. Maintain per-listing availability bitmaps for the search window (updated from the calendar service) and filter the candidate set with them before the nearest-neighbor search or in the same step. Constrain price to a band around the anchor listing (for example a bounded log-price ratio) and keep it in the same market and capacity class.

Ensure freshness: calendar changes must propagate within minutes, with a safety recheck at render time. If filtering leaves too few results, widen the radius or price band in steps and label the result as a relaxed match. Track unavailable-click rate and empty-carousel rate as product metrics and alert on them.

Likely follow-ups: What happens to latency when the filter removes 95 percent of candidates? · How do you keep the bitmap current at millions of updates per hour?

What makes this Staff-level: You put constraints where they save the most work and you monitor the user-visible failure.

The company is expanding to new markets with sparse supply and a new product category (experiences). What transfers, and what does not?

What transfers: the session-based embedding recipe, the evaluation harness, the cold-start fallback from metadata, and the operating playbook. What does not: market-specific co-click structure (a ski town and a city behave differently), the definition of "similar" (experiences are matched by time and activity, not by bedrooms), and the inventory constraints.

Plan it as a template: shared infrastructure and training code, market-level evaluation sets and negatives, metadata encoders that can start working with no history, and a launch checklist with exit criteria (calibration, coverage, empty-result rate). Staff it with a small platform group that owns the template and local owners for each market or category.

Likely follow-ups: How would you decide to train a global model versus per-market models? · What is your minimum data threshold to turn the model on?

What makes this Principal-level: You separate what to standardize from what to localize, and you turn expansion into a repeatable process.

New hosts rarely appear in the carousel. Is the model creating a rich-get-richer loop, and what do you do?

Check with data: impression share by listing age and by host tenure, the Gini coefficient of impressions, and how much of the exposure disparity remains after controlling for quality (reviews, price, photos). Co-click training favors listings that already have clicks, and popularity-proportional negative sampling reinforces it.

Mitigations: sample negatives with a flattened popularity distribution, give metadata-based embeddings for new listings, reserve a small exploration share of slots with a quality threshold, and cap how much any listing's exposure can be boosted. Measure the cost in near-term bookings and the benefit in supply health (host retention, time to first booking), then choose the exploration share on that trade-off.

Likely follow-ups: What is your guardrail so boosts do not show poor listings? · How do you measure supply-side benefit in an A/B test?

What makes this Staff-level: You measure the loop and the marketplace-level benefit, not only guest-side conversion.
