Chapter 8

Ad Click Prediction on Social Platforms

Ads in a social feed are sold in an auction where the advertiser pays only if the user clicks, so the platform needs a probability of click for every (user, ad, context) that it can multiply by a bid. That makes this an unusual ML problem: the value of the score, not just its order, moves money. The model must be calibrated, fast enough to score hundreds of ads in tens of milliseconds, and fresh enough to learn about ads that were created an hour ago. The core idea is a pointwise click model trained with log loss, wrapped in an explicit story for calibration, label delay, and sampling, and placed inside an auction.

Clarifying Requirements

Ad prediction questions hide a lot of business logic. Use the first minutes to pin down the pricing model, because it decides what the model must output.

Candidate

Which surface are we designing for, and how many ads per request?

Interviewer

Ads inserted into the main feed. Assume one ad slot is filled per ad opportunity, roughly one opportunity every few posts. Image, video, and carousel creatives all count as one ad format.

Candidate

How do advertisers pay, and how is the winner chosen?

Interviewer

Cost per click, in an auction. Each advertiser sets a maximum bid per click. The platform ranks eligible ads by expected value and charges the winner only when a click happens.

Candidate

So the model's output feeds the auction directly. Do we need calibrated probabilities, or is a good ordering enough?

Interviewer

Calibrated. Ordering alone is not enough, because the auction multiplies the score by a bid. Explain why in your design.

Candidate

What do we know at request time?

Interviewer

The user profile and engagement history, the ad creative and targeting metadata, and context such as device, placement, and time. There is no search query.

Candidate

What is the scale?

Interviewer

Assume 300 million daily active users, about ten ad opportunities per user per day, and around ten million live ads. Targeting leaves a few thousand eligible ads per request.

Candidate

What is the latency budget?

Interviewer

The whole ad stage gets about 50 ms at p99, inside the feed's own budget. A slow ad call delays the feed.

Candidate

Which feedback signals do we log, and how soon do they arrive?

Interviewer

Impressions and clicks with the features used at serving time. Clicks usually arrive within seconds to minutes. Conversions and hides exist but conversions are out of scope for the first version.

Candidate

How often do new ads and advertisers appear, and how fresh must the model be?

Interviewer

Thousands of new ads per hour. Campaigns change budgets and creatives all day, so the model should learn continuously, not weekly.

Candidate

Any constraints on data, safety, or fairness?

Interviewer

Respect user opt-outs and limited identifiers on some platforms. Invalid clicks must not be billed or learned from. Special ad categories such as housing and employment have restricted targeting.

Candidate

What is out of scope?

Interviewer

Auto-bidding for advertisers, creative generation, and multi-slot auction design. Mention them if time allows.

Build a click model for feed ads on a platform with 300 million daily users. For each (user, ad, context) it outputs a calibrated probability of click, which a cost-per-click auction multiplies by the bid to rank ads. Scoring must fit inside tens of milliseconds, handle thousands of new ads per hour, and keep learning continuously from impression and click logs.

Frame the Problem as an ML Task

ML objective

Several objectives sound plausible. The way to choose is to ask what the downstream consumer, the auction, needs from the model.

Candidate objectives
ObjectiveProsCons
Rank ads by P(click) onlySimple; maximizes engagementIgnores bids, so advertisers cannot compete and revenue is left on the table; no price can be derived
Regress expected revenue per impression (bid × click) directlyOne number to sort byBids change by the minute, so the target distribution shifts and the model must be retrained whenever bids move; heavy-tailed target; hides the click behavior you want to monitor
Predict a calibrated P(click) and let the auction combine it with bid and pacingSeparates stable user behavior (the model) from fast-moving prices (the auction); supports per-click pricing; debuggableAccuracy of the probability value, not only its order, now matters; needs a calibration story

Predict a calibrated probability of click and keep the bid out of the model. The model owns user behavior, the auction owns money. Everything else in this chapter (downsampling corrections, calibration layers, online learning, delay handling) exists to keep that probability honest.

Why does the value matter when only the ordering seems to drive the winner? Because the auction ranks by bid × pCTR, and a model that preserves the order of pCTR can still flip the order of the product. Take three ads competing for one slot. The true click probabilities are 1.0%, 3.0%, and 1.8%; suppose the model over-predicts only ad B, by 2x.

Worked auction example (eCPM = bid × pCTR × 1000, in dollars per thousand impressions)
AdBid per clickTrue pCTRTrue eCPMModel pCTRModel eCPM
A$2.001.0%$201.0%$20
B$0.503.0%$156.0%$30
C$1.001.8%$181.8%$18

With correct scores, A wins (20 vs 18 vs 15). Under a second-price rule the winner pays just enough to beat the runner-up, so A pays 18/0.01 = $1.80 per click and revenue is 0.01 × 1.80 = $0.018 per impression. With the miscalibrated model, B wins (30 vs 20 vs 18) and pays the runner-up's score divided by its own predicted pCTR: 0.020 / 0.06 = $0.333 per click. B really clicks at 3%, so revenue is 0.03 × 0.333 = $0.010 per impression, a 44% drop (0.018 → 0.010), and the ad shown also has the lower true value to the market. The pCTR ranking never changed; the calibration error did the damage.

Input and output

The model's input is the feature vector for one (user, ad, context) triple; its output is a probability in (0, 1). Around it sits the auction, which turns probabilities into a winner and a price. Keep this boundary clear in an interview: the ML system produces pCTR, and the auction adds the bid, a pacing multiplier, and a quality or reserve rule.

Pricing matters for the design. In a generalized second-price (GSP) rule, the winner at rank i pays, per click, the score of the next ad divided by its own pCTR: price_i = score_(i+1) / pCTR_i. A one-slot feed ad behaves like a second-price auction in the quality-adjusted score, and VCG variants charge each winner the harm it causes others. In every variant the price depends on other ads' pCTRs, so errors propagate into revenue and into what advertisers feel they are paying for.

ML category

The formulation is pointwise binary classification with a probabilistic output, trained with log loss. Ranking-oriented losses are tempting but wrong here, because they only care about order.

Formulations considered
FormulationFitWhy or why not
Pointwise binary classification (log loss)ChosenLog loss is a proper scoring rule: its minimizer is the true probability, which is exactly what the auction needs
Pairwise or listwise learning to rankRejected for the final scorerOptimizes order within a list and gives scores with no probability meaning; calibration would need a second model
Per-ad aggregate CTR regressionBaseline onlyIgnores the user, so it cannot personalize; useful as a smoothed prior for new ads
Contextual bandit on topAdd laterHandles exploration for new ads, but needs a calibrated base model with uncertainty first

The serving system is a cascade, as in the public Facebook description: cheap stages cut millions of ads to a manageable set, and only the last stage uses the expensive model. The counts below are assumptions for the scenario above.

In an interview, say that two-tower models belong in the pre-ranking and retrieval stages. The final scorer needs rich user-by-ad interactions (user history with this advertiser, creative type by device) and calibrated output, which a dot product of two independent embeddings cannot give.

Sizes (assumptions). 300 million users × 10 opportunities is about 3 billion impressions per day, or 34,700 impressions per second on average. With roughly 1 KB of logged features per impression, that is about 3 TB per day. At a 1% CTR there are about 30 million clicks per day. Logging served_pctr and model_ver costs almost nothing and enables calibration monitoring and counterfactual analysis later.

Labels come from joining two streams that arrive at different times. The join is a stateful stream job: impressions wait in a buffer keyed by request ID, clicks look them up, and an impression is emitted as a negative only after a waiting window has passed.

How long should the window W be? Clicks on feed ads arrive quickly, but not instantly, and the tail is long. The curve below is illustrative; measure your own surface, because the shape depends on the creative type and the landing flow.

Window choice (coverage from the illustrative curve; joiner state assumes 34,700 impressions/s and 1 KB each)

The buffer sizes follow from 34,700 × window seconds × 1 KB: 120 s gives 4.2 million records, 600 s gives 20.8 million, and 1,200 s gives 41.7 million. A short window biases labels toward negatives and makes the model under-predict, which is correctable if the coverage is stable. A long window delays learning and costs memory. The public Facebook write-up describes exactly this recency versus coverage balance and reports that practical windows reduce the bias to a small fraction of a percent. Two further tools exist for slow signals such as conversions: model the delay explicitly (Chapelle), or emit every impression immediately as a negative and re-emit a positive when a late click arrives, correcting the loss with importance weights (Ktena et al.).

A broken click stream looks like a model problem. If the click feed stalls, the joiner emits only negatives, the labeled CTR collapses toward zero, and an online learner happily learns to predict zero for everything. Alert on joiner input rates and on the labeled CTR, and have the trainer disconnect automatically when the label distribution moves abruptly.

Two findings from the Facebook paper are worth knowing. First, features describing history (the ad's recent CTR, the user's average CTR) dominated feature importance: the top ten features were all historical. A model with only contextual features was about 4.5% worse in normalized entropy than the full model, while one with only historical features was less than 1% worse. Second, contextual features still matter for cold start, because a new ad or user has no history to look up.

Encoding trade-offs. High-cardinality IDs (user, ad, advertiser) cannot be one-hot encoded. Feature hashing maps field:value into a fixed number of buckets, such as 222 = 4.2 million, with no vocabulary to maintain; new ads get a bucket automatically. The cost is collisions (unrelated IDs share a row) and no semantic meaning for the bucket. Learned embeddings give each ID a dense vector, which generalizes between similar IDs and feeds neural models, but the table is the dominant memory cost. Numeric features are bucketized so a linear layer can learn a non-linear response, and crossed features capture interactions a linear model cannot find on its own.

Embedding memory. Rows × dimension × bytes per value, in fp32. These sizes are assumptions chosen to be plausible for the scale.

user_id: 500,000,000 × 32 × 4 bytes = 64 GB. creative_id: 50,000,000 × 32 × 4 = 6.4 GB. ad_id: 20,000,000 × 32 × 4 = 2.56 GB. cross: 10,000,000 × 16 × 4 = 0.64 GB. advertiser_id: 5,000,000 × 16 × 4 = 0.32 GB. topic: 1,000,000 × 16 × 4 = 0.064 GB. Total is about 74 GB. Training with Adam keeps weights plus two moment estimates, so roughly three times that (about 220 GB); serving in fp16 halves it and 8-bit row-wise quantization cuts it to about a quarter. This is why the table is sharded across hosts and why teams hash user IDs into fewer buckets or drop raw user_id in favor of behavioral features.

Labels

Candidate label sources
LabelProsCons
Click within the windowDense enough, fast (seconds to minutes), directly what a CPC advertiser pays forIncludes accidental clicks and click bait; fraud contaminates it
Quality click (click plus landing-page dwell or follow-up action)Filters accidental clicks; closer to advertiser valueSlower, needs landing-page measurement, often unavailable off-platform

Decision. Train on clicks that pass a validity filter, and add conversions later as a second head. A click label with validity filtering is the fastest, densest signal and matches what is billed. Four practical issues follow.

  • Imbalance. CTR is about 1% or lower, so 99% of rows are negatives. Downsampling negatives cuts training cost by close to 10x, but it shifts the base rate, so predictions must be corrected before they reach the auction (see Model Development).
  • Noise. Bots, click farms, double clicks, and accidental taps produce positive labels with no intent. Filter before labeling and refund advertisers; both the learner and the bill must agree on which clicks are real.
  • Delay. Handled by the waiting window above, with a calibration layer absorbing the small remaining bias.
  • Selection and position bias. The log contains only ads that won earlier auctions, shown in particular slots. Ads in higher slots collect more clicks irrespective of quality. Log slot position as a feature, and keep a small exploration slice where ads are chosen with known probabilities so inverse-propensity weighting can correct offline estimates (Joachims et al.).

Split data by time, not at random. Train on earlier days and validate on later days, and never downsample the validation set. A random split leaks the same ad and user across both sides and hides the drift you will meet in production.

Keep GBDT+LR and plain logistic regression as the baseline and the fallback. In an interview, the safe path is to start from GBDT+LR because it is simple to explain and well documented, then argue for the deep model when the feature space and data volume justify the cost.

GBDT plus logistic regression

The Facebook hybrid first trains boosted trees in batch. Each tree maps an example to one leaf, and the leaf index is treated as a categorical feature with one-hot coding. A linear classifier on those one-hot leaves then learns a weight for every learned rule. If two trees have 3 and 2 leaves and an impression lands in leaf 3 of the first and leaf 2 of the second, the linear model sees [0, 0, 1, 0, 1]. The paper reported that gains flatten after about 500 trees with at most 12 leaves each, and that this combination beat either model alone by over 3% in normalized entropy. The linear layer can be updated online in minutes, while the trees refit daily.

The online linear layer is where the Google ad-click paper is useful. It trains logistic regression with FTRL-Proximal, which gives an L1-regularized, sparse model and a per-coordinate learning rate ηt,i = α / (β + sqrt(sum of squared gradients for feature i)). Rare features keep a large step and common features settle down, which matters because a feature that appeared twice should not be updated like one seen a billion times. The Facebook paper compared learning-rate schemes and found per-coordinate rates best, with about 5% lower NE than the worst per-weight scheme. The Google paper also gives memory tricks that remain useful: probabilistic inclusion of new features, 16-bit fixed-point weights, and progressive validation (score each example before learning from it) for a cheap online metric.

Deep model with explicit crosses

At larger scale a neural model replaces trees as the interaction learner. The Deep & Cross design feeds the same embedded input x0 into two branches. The cross branch applies layers of the form x_next = x0 · (W x + b) + x (an elementwise product with the original input), so each layer raises the polynomial degree of the learned interactions by one; three layers capture up to fourth-order crosses. The deep branch is an ordinary MLP for implicit interactions. DeepFM instead shares embeddings between a factorization machine, which handles second-order crosses, and an MLP. Wide & Deep keeps a hand-crossed linear model beside the MLP.

Training data construction and loss

Every impression is a training row. All positives are kept; negatives are downsampled with a keep-rate w. The Facebook experiments found the best normalized entropy at a negative keep-rate of 0.025 among the rates they tried, so the right w is a tuned hyperparameter, not a constant.

The loss is log loss: L = −[ y · log p + (1 − y) · log(1 − p) ]. In words, it charges the negative log of the probability assigned to what actually happened. A confident wrong answer costs a lot, and the minimizer is the true click rate, which is why log loss yields calibrated probabilities where a ranking loss does not. For a click (y = 1), predicting 0.02 costs −ln 0.02 = 3.91, while predicting 0.01 costs 4.61; for a non-click, predicting 0.01 costs only −ln 0.99 = 0.010.

Downsampling changes the base rate the model sees. If 1% of impressions click and only a fraction w of negatives is kept, the model learns a click rate that is much higher than reality. The correction follows from odds: sampled odds are true odds divided by w, so p = p' / (p' + (1 − p') / w), where p' is the model output on sampled data.

Take 1 million impressions at a true CTR of 1%: 10,000 clicks and 990,000 non-clicks. Keep w = 0.1 of the negatives, which leaves 99,000. The sampled click rate is 10,000 / 109,000 = 0.0917. A perfect model trained on this set outputs p' = 0.0917 for an average impression. The correction gives p = 0.0917 / (0.0917 + 0.9083 / 0.1) = 0.0917 / 9.1747 = 0.0100, the true 1%. Another point: a score p' = 0.30 maps to 0.30 / (0.30 + 0.70 / 0.1) = 0.30 / 7.30 = 4.1%. With w = 0.025 the same raw 0.30 maps to 0.30 / (0.30 + 28) = 1.06%.

An alternative is to keep the model in true-probability space by giving each kept negative a training weight of 1/w. The Google paper takes this importance-weighting route. It needs no correction at serving time, but it raises gradient variance, so the choice is a trade-off between simplicity at serve time and statistical efficiency at train time. Whichever you choose, add a final calibration layer (isotonic or piecewise-linear, per slice) fit on a recent, un-sampled slice of served traffic. Neural networks in particular drift toward over-confidence (Guo et al.), and a monotone map fixes this without changing the ranking. Train large sparse-embedding models in a single pass over streaming data and warm-start each day from the previous checkpoint; a second pass over the same rows often overfits those embeddings sharply.

Calibrate on the traffic that the auction actually serves. The auction picks winners from many noisy estimates, so it systematically selects ads whose scores are too high (the winner's curse). A model that is calibrated on all impressions can still over-predict on the winners. Monitor calibration on served impressions, by slice, and fit the final layer there.

Normalized entropy

NE is the average log loss per impression divided by the log loss of a trivial model that predicts the background CTR for every row; it equals one minus the relative information gain. Lower is better, and NE below 1 means the model beats the baseline. The division makes the metric insensitive to the base CTR: the closer the CTR is to 0 or 1, the easier it is to get a small raw log loss, and normalization removes that effect.

Ten impressions, two clicks, so the background CTR is 0.2. The baseline log loss is the entropy H(0.2) = −(0.2 ln 0.2 + 0.8 ln 0.8) = 0.3219 + 0.1785 = 0.5004. The model predicts 0.6 and 0.4 for the two clicks and 0.1 (four times), 0.2 (three times), and 0.3 (once) for the eight non-clicks. Click losses: −ln 0.6 + −ln 0.4 = 0.5108 + 0.9163 = 1.4271. Non-click losses: 4 × 0.1054 + 3 × 0.2231 + 0.3567 = 0.4214 + 0.6694 + 0.3567 = 1.4475. Total 2.8747, average 0.2875. NE = 0.2875 / 0.5004 = 0.574, so the relative information gain is about 43%. Mean prediction is 0.23 versus an observed 0.20, a calibration ratio of 1.15.

The numbers in the chart: for A, entropy of a 1% CTR is 0.0560, so a model log loss of 0.050 gives NE 0.893; for B the entropy is 0.5004, so a log loss of 0.48 gives NE 0.959. Raw log loss says A is ten times better; NE says the model on A is simply a bit better than its baseline, which is the fair comparison. NE also reflects calibration: the Facebook paper notes that if a model over-predicts by 2x and a global multiplier of 0.5 fixes it, NE improves while AUC does not move at all.

AUC and its blind spots

AUC counts, over all (clicked, non-clicked) pairs, the fraction ordered correctly. It has four blind spots in ads. It ignores calibration, because any monotone transform of the scores leaves it unchanged. It mixes two kinds of ordering, between users and between ads for the same user, and a model that only learns which users click a lot scores well while adding nothing to ad choice; group AUC addresses that. It treats all pairs equally, while the auction cares about the top of the list after weighting by bids. And it is too coarse for the small gains that matter at this scale, where an NE change of a fraction of a percent is a real result.

Clicked scores 0.04 and 0.02; non-clicked scores 0.03, 0.01, 0.005. The 0.04 click beats all three (3 pairs). The 0.02 click beats 0.01 and 0.005 but loses to 0.03 (2 of 3). AUC = 5/6 = 0.833. Now multiply every score by 5. The order is identical, so AUC is still 0.833, yet the model now predicts a CTR five times too high. A model like that wrecks the auction and passes an AUC-only review.

Calibration

The calibration ratio is the sum of predicted probabilities over the sum of observed clicks. If a model predicts 12,000 clicks over a day with 10,000 actual clicks, the ratio is 1.20, an over-prediction of 20%. Report it per slice (country, device, placement, ad age, advertiser size), because a global 1.0 can hide a 1.5 on new ads balanced by a 0.8 on old ones. A reliability diagram bins predictions and plots the observed rate in each bin against the bin's mean prediction; a calibrated model hugs the diagonal.

If asked "which offline metric would you ship on", answer with a set: NE for overall quality, calibration ratio per slice as a hard gate, group AUC as a diagnostic. Then add that offline metrics are computed on logs from the previous policy, so they predict online results only roughly and every launch still needs an A/B test.

Ads A/B tests have pitfalls that ordinary product tests lack. Users in control and treatment draw on the same advertiser budgets, so a treatment that spends faster steals impressions from control, and the measured lift is partly cannibalization. Advertisers also react: a better model changes bids and budgets over days. Pacing systems interact with the model, so a short test can look better simply because it spent more of an unpaced budget early in the day.

  • Split the budget, not only the users. Give each arm its own half of every advertiser's budget, or randomize at the campaign level, so spend is not shared. A pure user-level split is acceptable only when most budgets are not binding.
  • Run long enough for pacing and learning. At least a full week to cover weekday cycles, with a ramp so the online learner and pacing controllers settle.
  • Watch both sides of the market. Advertiser outcomes (return on spend, delivery) are outcomes, not only user metrics.
  • Keep a long-term holdout. A small, permanent holdout on the old model measures drift and slow effects such as ad fatigue.

Serving

The serving system has an online path that must answer in tens of milliseconds and an offline path that keeps the model and its features fresh. The two paths share exactly two things: the feature definitions and the model registry.

Latency and scale math

These figures are assumptions for the scenario, to show the reasoning an interviewer wants.

  • Request rate. 3 billion ad opportunities per day is 34,700 per second on average; assume a 3x peak, about 104,000 requests per second.
  • Scoring rate. 300 ads per request after pre-ranking gives 31 million model scorings per second at peak.
  • Compute. Assume 3 full-rank cross layers on a 1,000-dimensional input (3 × 1M multiply-adds) plus a 1024-512-256 MLP (about 1.7M): roughly 4.7M multiply-adds, or 9.4 MFLOP, per ad. At 31 million ads per second that is about 300 TFLOP/s. Matrix math is a few tens of accelerators; low-rank cross layers and sharing user-side work across the 300 ads cut it further.
  • Memory traffic is the real cost. With 40 embedding fields of 32 floats (128 bytes) each, 31 million scorings per second means 1.25 billion row lookups and about 160 GB/s of random reads, spread over many sharded hosts. Redundancy across regions and peak headroom dominate fleet size, not FLOPs.

The budget sums to 8 + 12 + 6 + 15 + 4 + 5 = 50 ms. Two techniques recover time when it is tight: batch all 300 candidates in a single model call, and fetch user features once per request rather than once per ad.

Refresh and stability

Freshness has a measurable payoff. In the Facebook experiments, accuracy fell steadily as the gap between training and test data grew, and training daily instead of weekly improved NE by about 1%. The cure is incremental training, but fast learning also amplifies noise, bad data, and feedback loops, so each update passes a gate.

Challenges

Two of these deserve a closer look because interviewers often probe them: cold-start ads and budget pacing.

Cold start. A new ad has no history, and its CTR estimate is noisy. For a true CTR of 1%, the standard error of an empirical estimate after n impressions is sqrt(p(1−p)/n), so the relative error is about sqrt(99/n).

The arithmetic: sqrt(99/100) = 0.995, sqrt(99/1,000) = 0.315, sqrt(99/10,000) = 0.099, and sqrt(99/100,000) = 0.031. A fresh ad therefore lives in a regime where the model's own estimate is mostly prior. The strategies below fill that gap.

Cold-start strategies
StrategyHow it worksRisk
Content and context featuresCreative embeddings, category, text, device; these carry signal with no historyWeaker than behavioral history
Hierarchical priorsSmooth ad CTR toward its campaign, advertiser, and category averages; for example (clicks + 2) / (impressions + 200)Prior too strong delays learning
Exploration budgetGive new ads a protected share of impressions (epsilon-greedy, or Thompson sampling using the posterior variance)Costs short-term revenue; must be capped
Uncertainty-aware scoreRank by an upper confidence bound for new ads, then fade it out as data accruesNeeds calibrated uncertainty
Advertiser-level transferUse the advertiser's other ads' behavior as featuresNew advertisers have none

Budget pacing. An advertiser with a daily budget can exhaust it in the morning if its ads simply win every auction they enter, missing the evening audience and starving later learning. A pacing controller spreads spend by multiplying the ad's score or entry probability with a factor adjusted from observed spend against a target curve. Apply pacing as a separate multiplier in the auction, never by distorting pCTR, so the model stays calibrated and the controller stays debuggable.

Other Talking Points

  • Multi-task CTR and CVR. Advertisers who optimize for conversions need P(click) × P(convert | click); training the conversion head only on clicked rows is biased, so models such as ESMM train both heads over all impressions and multiply them.
  • Position bias. Higher slots get more clicks regardless of quality; train with slot as a feature and serve with a fixed default, or use a separate position tower, so the model does not credit the slot to the ad.
  • Counterfactual evaluation. Logs only contain ads that won, so offline estimates are biased; keep a small randomized exploration slice and use inverse-propensity weighting to compare candidate models offline.
  • Privacy changes. Limits on tracking remove cross-app signals and delay or aggregate conversion data, so expect weaker historical features, more modeled conversions, and a larger role for contextual and first-party features.
  • Feedback loops. Ads shown more collect more data and higher confidence, while unshown ads stay unknown; exploration and uncertainty-aware scoring keep the system from locking in early winners.
  • Winner's curse. Selecting the maximum of noisy estimates over-predicts the winners; calibrate on served traffic and consider shrinking estimates by their uncertainty.
  • Fraud filtering. Invalid clicks poison labels and bills at the same time, so the filter belongs before the joiner output and its decisions must be logged and auditable.
  • Ad quality and user value. Add heads for hide and report probability and rank by bid × pCTR plus a user-value term, so a high bid cannot buy a bad experience.
  • Why not only a two-tower. Dot-product models are the right tool for retrieval and pre-ranking over millions of ads, but they cannot represent fine cross features or give reliably calibrated probabilities for pricing.
  • Reserve prices and ad load. A minimum score keeps low-value ads out, and the number of ad slots is itself a trade-off between revenue and user experience, tested with long-running experiments.

Check yourself

Questions are tagged by the level they target. The first set checks Senior-level fundamentals and arithmetic; Staff and Principal questions probe trade-offs, operations, experimentation and organization. Try to answer aloud for two minutes before opening one.

Why can a model with better AUC still lose revenue in the auction?AUC only measures order and is unchanged by any monotone rescaling of scores. The auction ranks by bid times pCTR and prices against the runner-up's score, so what matters is the value of each score. A model can order users and ads well yet over-predict specific ads, or be globally off by a constant factor. In the worked example, over-predicting one ad by 2x kept the pCTR order but moved the winner and cut revenue per impression from $0.018 to $0.010. Use NE and per-slice calibration as gates, and AUC only as a ranking sanity check.
Numeric: CTR is 2% and you keep 5% of negatives. The model outputs 0.30 on sampled data. What is the real pCTR?With w = 0.05: p = p' / (p' + (1 − p') / w) = 0.30 / (0.30 + 0.70 / 0.05) = 0.30 / (0.30 + 14) = 0.30 / 14.30 = 0.021, about 2.1%. Sanity check: the sampled base rate is 0.02 / (0.02 + 0.98 × 0.05) = 0.02 / 0.069 = 0.29, so a score of 0.30 is just above average, and 2.1% is just above the true 2%. If you forgot to correct, you would bill and rank as if the click chance were 30%.
How do you choose the label waiting window, and what happens if it is too short?Plot the cumulative arrival of clicks after impression and weigh coverage against delay and buffer cost. If 98.5% of clicks arrive within 10 minutes, then at a true CTR of 1% the labeled CTR is 0.985%, so the model under-predicts by 1.5% (correctable by a factor of about 1/0.985 = 1.015 if coverage is stable). Joiner state at 34,700 impressions per second with 1 KB each is about 21 GB for a 10-minute window. Too short a window mislabels late clicks as negatives and biases the model low; too long delays learning. For slow signals like conversions, model the delay or use the fake-negative and re-emit scheme with importance weights.
Why is a two-tower model a poor choice for the final ad scorer?It reduces the interaction between user and ad to a dot product of two embeddings computed independently, so it cannot represent rich crosses such as user history with this advertiser, or creative type by device and slot. It also gives no natural calibrated probability, which pricing requires. Its strength is that ad embeddings can be pre-computed and searched with ANN, so it fits retrieval and pre-ranking, where cost per ad must be tiny. The final stage scores only a few hundred ads, so a heavier interaction model is affordable there.
Numeric: a new ad_id field has 40 million IDs with 24-dimension embeddings. How much memory for serving in fp16 and for training with Adam in fp32?Serving in fp16: 40,000,000 × 24 × 2 bytes = 1.92 GB. Training in fp32 with Adam keeps weights and two moment estimates, three copies of 40,000,000 × 24 × 4 bytes = 3.84 GB each, so 11.52 GB. The table is sharded across hosts, with hot rows cached. Options to shrink it: hash IDs into fewer buckets, use smaller dimensions for rare IDs, 8-bit row-wise quantization for serving, or a sparse optimizer such as Adagrad that keeps one accumulator per row.
An A/B test of a new model shows CTR up 2% but revenue flat and hides up 10%. What do you conclude and do next?The model is finding clicks, not value: more clicks at similar revenue means the extra clicks are cheap (priced against lower runner-up scores) or low quality, and the hide increase says users dislike what is shown. Check calibration per slice and on winners first, since over-prediction on a segment pulls in poor ads. Check whether the arms shared budgets, which can make a CTR lift partly cannibalization. Look at advertiser outcomes and conversion quality. Do not ship on CTR alone; add a user-value or hide-probability term to the ranking score and rerun with a budget-split design.
pCTR is well calibrated overall but over-predicts by 30 percent for new advertisers. What is the impact and the fix?

In an auction, the score multiplies the bid, so over-predicted ads win slots they should not. Short term this can raise revenue (advertisers pay for clicks that never come), but it lowers advertiser return, causes churn, and distorts the market: the winner's curse in action. Overall calibration hides it because errors cancel across segments.

Fixes: add ad-age and advertiser-history features so the model knows it is uncertain; calibrate per segment (for example isotonic regression by ad-age bucket); shrink estimates toward a prior using the number of impressions; and give new ads an explicit exploration treatment instead of letting noisy estimates decide. Monitor expected calibration error by segment and alarm on any slice, not just the average.

Likely follow-ups: How do you calibrate a segment with very few examples? · What do you tell advertisers whose ads lose due to shrinkage?

What makes this Staff-level: You connect a calibration error to auction incentives and marketplace health.

You optimize for conversions, which arrive days after the click. How do you train with delayed labels?

Options: wait for a full attribution window (clean but stale), train on partial data (fresh but biased toward fast conversions), or train on fresh data with a correction. A common approach treats recent clicks as provisional negatives and uses a delay model or importance weights to correct the bias when a conversion arrives later; this keeps the model fresh without waiting. Choose the window from the empirical delay distribution, for example the time by which 95 percent of conversions have arrived.

Add privacy-era complications: aggregated or noisy conversion reporting means labels may be partial or delayed by design, so you predict conversions from modeled, aggregated signals and validate with randomized experiments. Monitor with a delay-corrected calibration check, since a naive check will look bad on recent data.

Likely follow-ups: How do you evaluate the model on data that is still maturing? · What do you do when the platform reports conversions only in aggregate?

What makes this Staff-level: You know the freshness-versus-bias trade-off and the effect of privacy changes on labels.

Design an experiment for an ads auction where advertisers share a budget across the control and treatment arms.

A user-level split is contaminated: an advertiser with a fixed budget spends more in one arm and therefore less in the other, so arms interfere through pacing. Better designs split the supply that is shared: randomize at the advertiser level, split each advertiser's budget between arms (two budget-controlled universes), or use geographic or time-based experiments for coarse changes.

Pick metrics for all three parties: platform revenue, advertiser value (return on ad spend, conversions per dollar) and user experience (hides, reports, time spent). Run long enough to cover pacing cycles and learning effects, keep a persistent holdout for long-term effects, and pre-register the decision rule. State the residual bias of your design and how you would detect it.

Likely follow-ups: What is the sample size cost of advertiser-level randomization? · How would you detect leakage between arms?

What makes this Principal-level: You identify the interference mechanism and choose the randomization unit to remove it.

Revenue drops 3 percent after a deploy; offline metrics were unchanged. How do you debug?

Work from cheapest checks to deepest: (1) sample ratio and traffic split: did the experiment assign correctly? (2) model and calibration versions in the serving path; (3) feature availability: null rates, default fill rates, and staleness of each feature; (4) compare logged serving-time features against what training saw for the same impressions (train-serve skew); (5) upstream schema or logging changes; (6) auction-side changes that interact with the new scores.

Mitigate first: roll back to the previous version while investigating. Prevent recurrence: log serving features and train on logged features, shadow-score new models before they take traffic, canary with automatic rollback on calibration and revenue alarms, run golden-request tests in CI, and monitor feature distributions with alerts per feature.

Likely follow-ups: Which check would you do first if the drop is concentrated in one placement? · How do you tell a bad model from a bad experiment?

What makes this Staff-level: You have a systematic, time-boxed debug order and you fix the system that let it happen.
